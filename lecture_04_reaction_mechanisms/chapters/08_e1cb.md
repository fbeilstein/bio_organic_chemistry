E1cB Reactions

First-order$^\*$ Unimolecular Reactions with Conjugate Base

---

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 38.33%;">

In contrast to the **E1 reaction**, which involves a **carbocation **intermediate, the **E1cB reaction** takes place through a **carbanion **intermediate. Base-induced abstraction of a proton in a slow, rate-limiting step gives an anion, which expels a leaving group on the adjacent carbon. The reaction is particularly common in substrates that have a poor leaving group, such as -OH, two carbons removed from a carbonyl group. The poor leaving group disfavors the alternative E1 and E2 possibilities, and the carbonyl group makes the adjacent hydrogen unusually acidic by resonance stabilization of the anion intermediate.

</div>

an elimination catalysed by a strong base (KOH), so it looks like E2. But the leaving group is hydroxide, which we categorically stated cannot be a leaving group in E2 eliminations.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.98%; top: 29.70%; width: 78.37%; height: 19.49%;">

Negative charges are stabilized by conjugation with carbonyl groups, the proton is therefore rather acidic (pKa about 20). This means that the base can remove it without the leaving group departing at the same time—the anion that results is stable enough to exist because it can be delocalized on to the carbonyl group.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 19.37%; top: 76.13%; width: 78.37%; height: 14.89%;">

Although the anion is stabilized by the carbonyl group, it still prefers to lose a leaving group and become an alkene. This step is also the rate-determining step of the elimination—the elimination is unimolecular, and so is some kind of E1 reaction.

</div>

</div>

The rate-determining elimination step in an E1cB reaction is unimolecular, so you might imagine it would have a first-order rate equation. But, in fact, the rate is also dependent on the concentration of base. This is because the unimolecular elimination involves the anion whose concentration is itself determined by the concentration of base by the equilibrium

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 66.96%; top: 31.78%; width: 32.81%; height: 25.75%;">

The rate is proportional to the concentration of the anion, and we now have an expression for that concentration. We can simplify it further because the concentration of water is constant.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.51%; top: 1.54%; width: 82.49%; height: 22.77%;">

**E1**: the leaving group goes first, and proton removal follows in a second step. 
**E2**: the two events happen at the same time, the proton is removed as the leaving group leaves.
**E1cB**: the proton removal moves in front of leaving group departure.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

E1cB reactions may be stereoselective—this one, for example, gives mainly the E-alkene product (2:1 with Z). The intermediate anion is planar, so the sterochemistry of the starting materials is irrelevant, the less sterically hindered (usually E) product is preferred.

</div>

![](./lecture_04_reaction_mechanisms/images/slide_77_img_153.png) {left=28.22 top=23.93 width=57.67 height=24.44}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.51%; top: 0.00%; width: 81.75%; height: 21.78%;">

With E1cB, the regioselectivity is straightforward: the location of the double bond is defined by the position of:
(a) the acidic proton and 
(b) the leaving group.

</div>

Some points about the table:
• Methyl halides cannot eliminate as there are no appropriately placed protons
• Increasing branching favours elimination over substitution and strongly basic hindered nucleophiles always eliminate unless there is no option
• Good nucleophiles undergo substitution by SN2 unless the substrate is tertiary and then the intermediate cation can eliminate by E1 as well as substitute by SN1

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 41.51%; top: 78.88%; width: 55.94%; height: 21.12%;">

• High temperatures favour elimination by gearing up the importance of entropy in the free energy of reaction (∆G = ∆H – T∆S). This is a good way of ensuring E1 in ambiguous cases

**E1cB in biochemistry (Fatty Acid Synthesis)**

A typical example occurs during the biosynthesis of fats when a 3-hydroxybutyryl
thioester is dehydrated to the corresponding unsaturated (crotonyl) thioester.
The base in this reaction is a histidine amino acid in the enzyme, and loss of the
OH group is assisted by simultaneous protonation.
