Each **motoneuron **that leaves the spinal cord innervates multiple muscle fibers, with the number of fibers innervated depending on the type of muscle. All the muscle fibers innervated by a single nerve fiber are called a **motor unit.** Small muscles that react rapidly and whose control must be exact may have  as few as 2-3 muscle fibers per motor unit (in some of the laryngeal muscles). Large muscles that do not require fine control may have several hundred muscle fibers in a motor unit (the soleus muscle). The muscle fibers in each motor unit are not completely independent but overlap other motor units in microbundles of 3 to 15 fibers.

**Slow Fibers (Type 1, Red Muscle)**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 18.30%; width: 80.35%; height: 74.18%;">

1. Slow fibers are smaller than fast fibers.

2. Slow fibers are also innervated by smaller nerve fibers.

3. Slow fibers have a more extensive blood vessel system and more capillaries to supply extra amounts of oxygen compared with fast fibers,

4. Slow fibers have greatly increased numbers of mitochondria to support high levels of oxidative metabolism.

5. Slow fibers contain large amounts of myoglobin, an iron-containing protein similar to hemoglobin in red blood cells. Myoglobin combines with oxygen
and stores it until needed, which also greatly speeds oxygen transport to the mitochondria. The myoglobin gives the slow muscle a reddish appearance—
hence, the name red muscle.

</div>

</div>

**Myoglobin **

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 13.41%; top: 11.18%; width: 53.56%; height: 80.20%;">

Small, oxygen-binding protein found mainly in muscle tissue.
Stores and releases O₂ to meet high metabolic demands during muscle activity.
Single polypeptide chain (~153 amino acids) with a heme group containing Fe²⁺.
Similar in structure to a subunit of hemoglobin, but monomeric.
Exhibits hyperbolic O₂-binding curve → high oxygen affinity, no cooperativity.
Facilitates oxygen diffusion from blood (hemoglobin) to mitochondria.
Abundant in slow-twitch (red) muscle fibers → enhances endurance.
Myoglobinuria (presence in urine) indicates muscle damage (e.g., rhabdomyolysis).

</div>

</div>

**Fast Fibers (Type II, White Muscle)**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 17.41%; width: 80.35%; height: 77.45%;">

1. Fast fibers are large for great strength of contraction.

2. Fast fibers have an extensive sarcoplasmic reticulum for rapid release of calcium ions to initiate contraction.

3. Large amounts of glycolytic enzymes are present in fast fibers for rapid release of energy by the glycolytic process.

4. Fast fibers have a less extensive blood supply than slow fibers because oxidative metabolism is of secondary importance.

5. Fast fibers have fewer mitochondria than slow fibers, also because oxidative metabolism is secondary. A deficit of red myoglobin in fast muscle gives it the
name white muscle.

</div>

</div>

ST/FT is Genetically determined but it varies widely (e.g. elite marathoners ~70–80% ST, sprinters ~70–80% FT).
Partially adaptable:
Training can shift properties, but not fully convert fiber types.
Endurance training increases oxidative capacity of FT fibers → they become more fatigue-resistant (“type IIa” hybrid fibers).
Strength/sprint training enhances glycolytic enzymes and cross-sectional area of FT fibers.
Complete transformation (I ↔ II) is limited and mostly seen under extreme conditions (denervation, hormonal changes, or long-term electrical stimulation in lab models).
Mitochondria, capillaries, enzymes, and myoglobin content are highly plastic, even if fiber type itself is genetically constrained.

Red meat (like beef, lamb, or duck) comes from muscles with many red fibers, hence high myoglobin content.
White meat (like chicken breast or fish) comes from fast-twitch fibers, which have less myoglobin and rely more on anaerobic metabolism.

When cooked, myoglobin changes color (left to right): In raw meat, the oxygen-carrying myoglobin is red; in cooked meat, the oxidized, denatured form of myoglobin is brown; in meats cured with nitrite, including corned beef and ham, the myoglobin assumes a stable pink form (NO is nitric oxide, a product of nitrite); and in uncured meats cooked in a charcoal grill or gas oven, traces of carbon monoxide (CO) accumulate and produce another stable pink form.

**1.** Establish standing reach height. The subject, standing with the preferred shoulder adjacent to a wall and feet flat on the floor, reaches as high as possible to touch the wall. The starting point (standing reach height) represents the distance from the wall mark (middle finger) to the floor, recorded in centimeters.
**2.** Bend the knees to about a 90° angle while moving the arms back in a winged position.
**3.** Thrust forward and upward, touching as high as possible on the wall.
**4.** At a minimum, perform three trials of the jump test, using the highest score as the vertical height. An average of the last 3 trials of 10 provides a more dependable jump height.
**5.** Compute vertical jump height (cm) as the difference between standing reach height and vertical height achieved in the jump.

**Immediate anaerobic power output**

PAPw =  60.7 (VJcm) + 45.3 (BMkg) - 2055

power output in watts (PAPw)

body mass in kilograms (BMkg)
