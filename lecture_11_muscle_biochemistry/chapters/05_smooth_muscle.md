Smooth muscle varies among organs in:
Size and organization
Type of stimuli it responds to
Pattern of innervation
Functional role
For simplicity, it is divided into **two main types**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.24%; top: 42.67%; width: 36.89%; height: 59.15%;">

**Multi-Unit Smooth Muscle**
Composed of **independent fibers**, each innervated by a **single nerve ending**
**No gap junctions** → fibers contract separately
Control mainly by **nerve signals**
Surrounded by thin basement-membrane-like layer (collagen + glycoprotein)
**Examples:**
Ciliary muscle (eye focusing)
Iris muscle (pupil size)
Piloerector muscles (hair erection)

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 50.00%; top: 55.64%; width: 50.00%; height: 44.37%;">

**Unitary (Single-Unit / Visceral) Smooth Muscle**
Consists of **hundreds to thousands of fibers** contracting as one unit
Connected by **gap junctions** → ions and action potentials spread easily
Arranged in **sheets or bundles**
Controlled mainly by **non-nervous stimuli** (e.g., stretch, hormones)
Found in **walls of visceral organs: **GI tract, bile ducts, ureters, uterus, blood vessels

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](./lecture_11_muscle_biochemistry/images/slide_77_img_97.png) {left=70.98 top=0.00 width=29.02 height=94.07}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.41%; top: 10.29%; width: 53.08%; height: 89.71%;">

**No striations:** Actin and myosin not organized into sarcomeres.
**Dense bodies:** Actin filaments attach to dense bodies (analogous to Z-disks).
Some connected to membrane, others inside cell.
Intercellular bridges link dense bodies of neighboring cells → transmit force.
**Filament ratio:** ~5–10× more actin than myosin.
**Side-polar myosin filaments:** Cross-bridges on opposite sides pull in opposite directions.
**Result:** Smooth muscle can shorten up to **80% of its length** (vs <30% in skeletal muscle).

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 1.68%; width: 38.73%; height: 96.44%;">

**Smooth vs. Skeletal Muscle Contraction**
**Contraction Characteristics**
**Smooth muscle:** slow, tonic contractions lasting seconds to days.
**Skeletal muscle:** rapid, phasic contractions.
**Cross-Bridge Cycling**
Much **slower** in smooth muscle (1/10–1/300 of skeletal).
**Low ATPase activity →** slower ATP hydrolysis and movement.
**Energy Use**
Requires only **1/10–1/300** as much energy to sustain tension.
Enables **long-term contraction** in viscera with minimal energy.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 56.31%; top: 5.54%; width: 43.69%; height: 94.46%;">

**Contraction Dynamics**
**Slow onset:** 50–100 ms after excitation.
**Total contraction time:** 1–3 s (vs. ~0.1 s in skeletal).
**Force**
Despite fewer myosin filaments, **greater max force** (4–6 kg/cm² vs. 3–4 kg/cm²).
Due to prolonged actin–myosin attachment.
**Latch Mechanism**
Maintains force with **minimal energy** and **reduced excitation** — ideal for tonic contraction.
**Stress–Relaxation Response**
After stretch, tension **returns to baseline** within 15–60 s.
Allows hollow organs (e.g. bladder) to **maintain pressure** despite volume changes.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](./lecture_11_muscle_biochemistry/images/slide_79_img_98.png) {left=65.85 top=2.96 width=34.15 height=81.21}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.08%; top: 1.48%; width: 52.58%; height: 100.00%;">

In place of troponin, smooth muscle cells contain a large amount of calmodulin. Calmodulin initiates contraction by activating the myosin cross bridges. 
**1.** The calcium concentration in the cytosolic fluid increases as a result of the influx through calcium channels and/or release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
**2.** The calcium ions bind reversibly with calmodulin.
**3.** The calmodulin-calcium complex then joins with and activates myosin light chain kinase, a phosphorylating enzyme.
**4.** One of the light chains of each myosin head, called the regulatory chain, becomes phosphorylated in response to this myosin kinase and obtains the capability of binding repetitively with the actin filament and proceeding through the entire cycling process of intermittent pulls, the same as what occurs for skeletal muscle, thus causing muscle contraction.

</div>

</div>

**Source of Ca²⁺ in Smooth Muscle**
Smooth muscle contraction **depends mainly on extracellular Ca²⁺ ** → sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) is **poorly developed**.
Ca²⁺ enters **through membrane channels** during an **action potential or stimulus**.
[Ca²⁺] outside ≈ 10⁻³ M, inside ≈ 10⁻⁷ M → strong diffusion gradient.
**Latency before contraction:** 200–300 ms → ~50× slower than skeletal muscle.
**Role of SR and Caveolae**
SR forms a **rudimentary network** near the membrane.
**Caveolae** = small membrane invaginations acting as analogs of **T-tubules**.
Action potentials in caveolae trigger **Ca²⁺ release** from adjacent SR.
More developed SR → **faster contraction**.
**Smooth muscle force strongly depends** on extracellular Ca²⁺ concentration. → Contraction ceases if [Ca²⁺]ₑ falls below 1/3–1/10 normal.

</div>

**Relaxation Mechanisms**
**Ca²⁺ pump** (ATP-dependent) removes Ca²⁺ to ECF or SR → **slow process**. → Explains long-lasting contraction (seconds vs. milliseconds).
**Myosin phosphatase** removes phosphate from myosin light chain → stops cross-bridge cycling.
Speed of relaxation depends on **enzyme activity level**.
**Latch Mechanism Regulation**
Allows **prolonged tension with minimal energy use**.
When **myosin kinase** and **phosphatase** are both active → fast cycling.
When activity decreases → heads stay **attached longer**, fewer ATP cycles.
Result: → **Force maintained (latched)** → **Energy conserved (low ATP use)**

</div>

**Endings of postganglionic autonomic neurons on smooth muscle.**

</div>

![](./lecture_11_muscle_biochemistry/images/slide_82_img_101.png) {left=44.64 top=12.37 width=55.36 height=60.53}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.41%; top: 11.18%; width: 35.39%; height: 61.05%;">

The nerve fibers run along the membranes of the smooth muscle cells and sometimes groove their surfaces. The multiple branches of postganglionic neurons are beaded with enlargements (varicosities) and contain synaptic vesicles. Neurotransmitter is released from the varicosities and diffuses to receptors on smooth muscle cell plasma membranes.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.41%; top: 72.90%; width: 83.58%; height: 27.53%;">

**In the heart**, cholinergic and noradrenergic nerve fibers end on the sinoatrial node, the atrioventricular node, and the bundle of His. Noradrenergic fibers also innervate the ventricular muscle. The exact nature of the endings on nodal tissue is not known. In the ventricle, the contacts between the noradrenergic fibers and the cardiac muscle fibers resemble those found in smooth muscle.

</div>

**Neuromuscular Junctions in Smooth Muscle**
Smooth muscle **lacks specialized motor end plates**.
**Autonomic nerve fibers** branch diffusely over muscle sheets.
Neurotransmitters are released into the **extracellular matrix**, not directly onto membranes.
**Diffuse junctions:** transmitter diffuses several nanometers–micrometers to reach cells.
Inner layers receive signal by **conduction or diffusion** from outer layers.
**Varicosities and Neurotransmitter Release**
Axons have **multiple varicosities** instead of terminal boutons.
At varicosities, **Schwann cells open**, allowing neurotransmitter release.
**Vesicles contain:** → Acetylcholine (ACh) *or* Norepinephrine (NE) — never both.
Some contain **other modulators** (e.g. peptides).
**Contact junctions** (20–30 nm gap) act like skeletal synapses → **faster contraction**.

</div>

![](./lecture_11_muscle_biochemistry/images/slide_83_img_102.png) {left=58.47 top=0.00 width=39.69 height=65.39}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 60.14%; top: 64.59%; width: 38.02%; height: 35.41%;">

Unitary (visceral) smooth muscle cells are connected by gap junctions so that depolarization can rapidly spread. In multi-unit smooth muscle, each cell is stimulated independently by a neurotransmitter released from closely associated autonomic nerve varicosities.

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 47.38%; width: 84.64%; height: 52.62%;">

**ACh and NE** are the key neurotransmitters.
**Never secreted by the same fibers.**
Their effects **depend on receptor type**:
**Excitatory receptors** → depolarization & contraction.
**Inhibitory receptors** → hyperpolarization & relaxation.
Example:
In some organs, ACh → excite; NE → inhibit.
In others, opposite effect.
**Outcome determined by receptor subtype**, not neurotransmitter itself.

</div>
