<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.08%; top: 1.38%; width: 37.45%; height: 97.33%;">

**Step 1:** Generation of an action potential in the motor neuron causes the small, saclike vesicles within the terminal axon to release acetylcholine (ACh). ACh diffuses across the synaptic cleft and attaches to specialized ACh receptors on the sarcolemma. Almost perfect symmetry exists between the “imprint” of the presynaptic vesicles that contain ACh and the “imprint” of the postsynaptic receptors that capture ACh.
**Step 2:** The muscle action potential depolarizes the transverse tubules at the sarcomere’s A–I junction. Step 3: Depolarization of the T-tubule system causes Ca2+ release from the lateral sacs (terminal cisternae) of the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
**Step 4:** Ca2+ binds to troponin–tropomyosin in the actin filaments. This releases the inhibition that prevented actin from combining with myosin.
**Step 5:** During muscle action, actin combines with myosin–ATP. Actin also activates the enzyme myosin ATPase, which then splits ATP. The reaction’s energy produces myosin crossbridge movement and creates tension.
**Step 6:** ATP binds to the myosin crossbridge; this breaks the actin–myosin bond and allows the crossbridge to dissociate from actin. The thick and thin filaments then slide past each other and the muscle shortens.
**Step 7:** Crossbridge activation continues when Ca2+ concentration remains high enough (from membrane depolarization) to inhibit the troponin–tropomyosin system.
**Step 8:** When muscle stimulation ceases, intracellular Ca2+ concentration rapidly decreases as Ca2+ moves back into the lateral sacs of the sarcoplasmic reticulum through active transport that requires ATP hydrolysis.
**Step 9:** Ca2+ removal restores the inhibitory action of troponin–tropomyosin. In the presence of ATP, actin and myosin remain in the dissociated, relaxed state.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.17%; width: 80.35%; height: 92.58%;">

In eukaryotic cells, motor proteins act on structural elements such as actin filaments and microtubules to generate the movements that allow the cells to reorganize their contents, change their shape, and even crawl or swim. For example, muscle contraction is accomplished by the motor protein myosin pulling on actin filaments.

The proteins on which we will focus in this lecture move along actin and microtubules— protein filaments composed of repeating subunits. 

The motor proteins cycle between forms having high or low affinity for the filament tracks in response to ATP binding and hydrolysis, enabling a bind, pull, and release mechanism that generates motion.

**Eukaryotic cells contain three major families of motor proteins: **

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 11.18%; width: 80.35%; height: 86.05%;">

**Myosins.** First characterized on the basis of its role in muscle. Move along filaments of the protein actin. Each molecule of muscle myosin consists of two copies each of a heavy chain with a molecular mass of 220 kDa, an essential light chain, and a regulatory light chain. The human genome encodes more than 40 distinct myosins; some function in muscle contraction, and others participate in a variety of other processes. 

**Kinesins**. have roles in protein, mRNA, and vesicle transport as well as construction of the mitotic spindle and chromosome segregation, are generally dimers of two polypeptides. The human genome encodes more than 40 kinesins.

**Dyneins.** Power the motion of cilia and flagella, and a general cytoplasmic dynein contributes to a variety of motions in all cells, including vesicle transport and various transport events in mitosis. Dyneins are enormous, with heavy chains of molecular mass greater than 500 kDa. The human genome encodes approximately 10 dyneins.

**Structural Similarities of Motor Proteins**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 20.32%; width: 80.35%; height: 70.69%;">

Early sequence comparisons of myosins, kinesins, and dyneins showed little similarity.
3D structures later revealed remarkable structural parallels between myosin and kinesin.
Both contain P-loop NTPase cores, similar to those in G proteins.
Dynein belongs to the AAA subfamily of P-loop NTPases (like in the 19S proteasome).
Dynein’s heavy chain has six P-loop NTPase domains, four of which bind nucleotides.
These shared motifs suggest related molecular mechanisms across all three motor families.

**Myosin Filaments**
**Myosin molecule:**
Molecular weight ≈ 480 kDa
2 heavy chains (≈ 200 kDa each) + 4 light chains (≈ 20 kDa each)
Heavy chains → twisted tail + two globular heads
Light chains → regulate head function
**Myosin filament structure:**
≈ 200 myosin molecules per filament (≈ 1.6 µm long)
Central region → tails bundled (tail core)
Heads project outward → **cross-bridges**
Each cross-bridge has two flexible **hinges** allowing motion
**Spatial arrangement:**
No cross-bridges in central 0.2 µm region
Heads rotated 120° around the filament → full coverage
**Functional feature:**
Myosin head acts as an **ATPase**, splitting ATP to power contraction

**Actin, Tropomyosin & Troponin**
**Actin filament:**
Backbone: double helix of F-actin (polymers of G-actin)
Each G-actin (≈ 42 kDa) binds ADP → active site for myosin head
Active sites repeat every ≈ 2.7 nm
Anchored in Z-disks; ≈ 1 µm long
**Tropomyosin:**
Long protein (≈ 70 kDa, 40 nm) wound around F-actin
In rest → covers active sites → prevents myosin binding
During contraction → moves to uncover active sites
**Troponin complex:**
3 subunits:
TnI – binds actin
TnT – binds tropomyosin
TnC – binds Ca²⁺
Ca²⁺ binding to TnC → conformational change → moves tropomyosin → starts contraction

**Sliding Filament Mechanism of Muscle Contraction**
**Relaxed State: ** Actin filaments from adjacent Z-disks barely overlap.
**Contracted State: **Actin filaments slide inward between myosin filaments until they overlap maximally.  Z-disks are pulled toward the center of the sarcomere.
**What Drives the Sliding?**
**Cross-bridges** on myosin interact with **actin** to generate force.
At rest, these forces are **inactive**.
When an **action potential** reaches the muscle fiber:
The **sarcoplasmic reticulum releases Ca²⁺**,
Ca²⁺ activates actin–myosin interactions
**Energy Source**
The process requires **ATP**.
**ATP → ADP + energy**, powering cross-bridge movement and filament sliding.

**Power stroke of myosin in skeletal muscle. A)** At rest, myosin
heads are bound to adenosine diphosphate and are said to be in a “cocked” position in relation to the thin filament, which does not have Ca2+ bound to the troponin-tropomyosin complex. **B)** Ca2+ bound to the troponin-tropomyosin complex induces a conformational change in the thin filament that allows for myosin heads to cross-bridge with thin filament actin. **C)** Myosin heads rotate, move the attached actin, and shorten the muscle fiber, forming the power stroke. **D)** At the end of the power stroke, ATP binds to a now exposed site and causes a detachment from the actin filament. **E)** ATP is hydrolyzed into ADP and inorganic phosphate (Pi) and this chemical energy is used to “re-cock” the
myosin head

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.75%; top: 1.98%; width: 48.74%; height: 48.02%;">

ATP Binding → Actin–Myosin Detachment
Tight ATP binding releases myosin from actin, using the free energy of ATP binding.

Search Phase (Actin⁻ State)
Myosin lever arm relaxes from its high-energy “up” position.
The detached head performs a biased Brownian search for a new actin site.
ATP hydrolysis occurs during this phase.

Rebinding and Powerstroke
Upon finding a new site, myosin binds weakly → then strongly.
Tight binding releases ADP·Pi, driving the lever-arm swing (powerstroke) that moves the cargo.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.75%; top: 47.38%; width: 21.03%; height: 52.31%;">

Load Dependence:
- Heavy load: slows ADP release → increases duty ratio → slower movement.
- Light load: faster ADP release → longer steps and faster motion.

The mechanism explains actomyosin energy coupling without invoking large internal deformations, instead relying on thermodynamic partitioning of ATP energy.

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 1.98%; width: 80.35%; height: 93.18%;">

Myosins turn over ~1 ATP per second → unusually slow for an enzyme.

18O Labeling Experiment
ATP hydrolysis studied using H₂¹⁸O (water with heavy oxygen).
Product phosphate contained 2–3 oxygens from water, not just 1.
**Conclusion:** ATP hydrolysis is reversible inside the myosin active site.

Enzyme-Bound Equilibrium
Free ATP hydrolysis: K ≈ 1.4 × 10⁵ (strongly favorable).
On enzyme: K ≈ 10 → near equilibrium between ATP and ADP·Pi.
**Reason:** enzymes stabilize the transition state, equalizing reactant/product energies.

Rate-Limiting Step
Hydrolysis itself ≠ rate-limiting.
Product release, especially Pi release, is rate-limiting.

Functional Implication
Myosin often holds ADP + Pi before release.
This persistence allows tight coupling between chemical energy and mechanical movement.
