<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.41%; top: 0.00%; width: 36.90%; height: 87.38%;">

The impulse arriving in the end of the motor neuron increases the permeability of its endings to Ca2+, which enters the endings and triggers exocytosis of the acetylcholine (ACh)-containing synaptic vesicles. ACh diffuses and binds to nicotinic cholinergic (NM) receptors in the motor endplate, which increases Na+ and K+ conductance. The resultant influx of Na+ produces the endplate potential. The current sink created by this local potential depolarizes the adjacent muscle membrane to its firing level. Action potentials are generated on either side of the endplate and are conducted away from the endplate in both directions along the muscle fiber and the muscle contracts. ACh is then removed from the synaptic cleft by acetylcholinesterase.

</div>

**Quantal Release at the Neuromuscular Junction**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 12.36%; width: 80.35%; height: 83.09%;">

Each nerve impulse releases ~60 synaptic vesicles, each containing ~10,000 ACh molecules.
At rest, small quanta of ACh are released randomly → miniature endplate potentials (MEPPs) ≈ 0.5 mV.
Ca²⁺ ↑ concentration at the endplate → ↑ size of quanta released
Mg²⁺ ↑ concentration at the endplate → ↓ size of quanta released
A nerve impulse triggers massive vesicle release → endplate potential exceeds threshold, causing muscle contraction.
Quantal release also occurs at noradrenergic, glutamatergic, and other synapses.
Disorders affecting this process:
Myasthenia gravis
Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome

</div>

</div>

**Myasthenia Gravis**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 12.96%; width: 80.35%; height: 83.09%;">

Autoimmune disease causing skeletal muscle weakness and fatigue
Incidence: 25–125 / million
Bimodal age distribution:
20s → mainly women
60s → mainly men
Major symptom: fatigue that worsens with activity

**Mechanism**
Antibodies attack nicotinic ACh receptors at the neuromuscular junction
→ Receptor destruction and endocytosis of clusters
↓ ACh receptors (−70–90%)
↓ Postsynaptic response to ACh
Structural changes: shallow, widened, or missing synaptic clefts

</div>

</div>

**Clinical & Genetic Features**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 14.14%; width: 80.35%; height: 81.90%;">

Two main forms:
Ocular → eye muscles
Generalized → limb + respiratory muscles
Fatigue worsens with repeated stimulation
May associate with:
Rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, polymyositis
Thymic abnormalities:
Hyperplasia (most)
Thymoma (10–15%)
~30% have family history of autoimmune disease

**Treatment**

Rest or acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (e.g. neostigmine, pyridostigmine) → ↑ ACh at synapse
Immunosuppressants: prednisone, azathioprine, cyclosporine
Thymectomy:
If thymoma present
Even without thymoma → 35% remission, 45% improvement

**Lambert–Eaton Myasthenic Syndrome (LEMS)**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 13.85%; width: 80.35%; height: 86.15%;">

**Cause:**
Autoimmune attack on voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels at presynaptic nerve endings → ↓ Ca²⁺ influx → ↓ acetylcholine release
**Epidemiology:**
~1 / 100,000 people (adult onset)
Men ≈ Women
**Clinical Features:**
Weakness in proximal lower limb muscles → waddling gait, difficulty lifting arms
Improves with repetitive stimulation (↑ Ca²⁺ accumulation) ↔ opposite of myasthenia gravis
**Associations:**
~40% cases with small-cell lung cancer
May also occur with thymoma or other cancers (lymphosarcoma, breast, colon, etc.)
Can be induced by aminoglycoside antibiotics
**Treatment:**
Treat underlying cancer (if present)
Immunotherapy: prednisone, plasmapheresis, IV immunoglobulin
Aminopyridines: ↑ ACh release (block K⁺ channels → activate Ca²⁺ channels)
AChE inhibitors: limited effect

</div>

</div>

During development, myoblasts (muscle precursor cells) fuse together. This fusion creates one long, multinucleated cell — a muscle fiber that can contain hundreds of nuclei. Action potential (step 7 prev. slide) travels into the muscle fiber interior through T-tubules.
Muscle (organ) → made of many muscle fibers (cells)
Muscle fiber (cell) → contains many myofibrils
Myofibril → made up of repeating sarcomeres
Sarcomere → contains actin (thin) and myosin (thick) filaments

Excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscle. The right panel shows an action potential in the transverse tubule that causes a conformational change in the voltage-sensing dihydropyridine (DHP) receptors, opening the ryanodine (RyR) Ca2+ release channels in the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum and permitting Ca2+ to diffuse rapidly into the sarcoplasm and initiate muscle contraction. **Malignant Hyperthermia (MH)**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 13.55%; width: 80.35%; height: 83.38%;">

A life-threatening hypermetabolic reaction of skeletal muscle triggered by certain drugs in genetically susceptible individuals.
It’s caused by uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
**Triggers:** Volatile anesthetics(Halothane, Sevoflurane, Desflurane, Isoflurane), Depolarizing muscle relaxant (Succinylcholine - the most classic trigger). These agents abnormally activate the ryanodine receptor (RyR1) or dihydropyridine receptor, leading to massive Ca²⁺ release inside muscle cells.
**Reason:** mutation in RYR1 (ryanodine receptor type 1) or less commonly CACNA1S (DHPR α1 subunit). Result → Ca²⁺ floods the cytosol → sustained contraction → heat, acidosis, and cell damage.
**Treatment: **Immediate administration of dantrolene, a RyR1 antagonist that stops Ca²⁺ release, symptomatic (cool the patient, correct acidosis and electrolyte imbalance).
Incidence ≈ 1:10,000–1:250,000 anesthetic procedures.
Carriers are usually asymptomatic until exposed to triggering agents.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.41%; top: 46.49%; width: 84.59%; height: 53.51%;">

**Single twitch**: A single action potential produces one brief contraction and relaxation.
**Summation of contractions**: When stimuli arrive before the muscle fully relaxes, Ca²⁺ remains elevated — producing stronger contractions.
**Incomplete tetanus**: Rapid, repeated stimuli cause contractions to partially fuse, with small relaxation intervals.
**Complete tetanus**: Very high stimulation frequency causes continuous, maximal tension with no relaxation phase.
Mechanism: Increased intracellular Ca²⁺ keeps troponin–tropomyosin complex shifted, allowing continuous cross-bridge cycling.
Physiological role: Allows smooth, sustained muscle contractions for posture and movement.

</div>
