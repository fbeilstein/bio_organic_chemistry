**Efficiency of Muscle Contraction.**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 14.74%; width: 80.35%; height: 78.93%;">

The percentage of the input energy to muscle (the chemical energy in nutrients) that can be converted into work, even under the best conditions, is **less than 25%**, with the remainder becoming heat. 

The reason for this low efficiency is that about one-half of the energy in foodstuffs is lost during the formation of ATP and, even then, only 40% to 45% of the energy in ATP itself can later be converted into work. 

Depends on contraction velocity:
**Slow contraction**: small amounts of maintenance heat are released during contraction, even though little or no work is performed, thereby decreasing the conversion efficiency to as little as zero.
**Moderate velocity (about 30% of maximum): maximal efficiency.**
**Rapid contraction: **much of the energy is used to overcome viscous friction within the muscle itself, and this too reduces the efficiency of contraction.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 19.19%; width: 40.57%; height: 75.72%;">

**Energy uses during contraction**
Cross-bridge cycling (“walk-along” mechanism)
Ca²⁺ reuptake into sarcoplasmic reticulum
Na⁺/K⁺ pumping to maintain membrane potential
**ATP supply**
Stored ATP ≈ 4 mM → lasts **1–2 s** of maximal contraction
ADP is rapidly rephosphorylated to ATP by three energy systems:
** 1. Phosphocreatine System**
**Phosphocreatine (PCr)** stores high-energy phosphate bonds
Transfers phosphate to ADP → regenerates ATP instantly
Supply lasts **~5–8 s** of maximal effort
Provides **immediate energy** (sprints, power lifts)

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 60.50%; top: 19.19%; width: 39.50%; height: 78.30%;">

**2. Glycolytic System**
**Breakdown of glycogen → pyruvate/lactate + ATP**
Works **without oxygen** (anaerobic)
~2.5× faster ATP formation than oxidative metabolism
Supports activity for **up to ~1 min**, but lactic acid accumulation limits duration
**3. Oxidative Metabolism**
**Oxygen + pyruvate/fatty acids/amino acids → ATP**
Supplies **>95%** of ATP for long-term contraction
Dominates during **aerobic, sustained exercise**
**Fuel sources:**
Short-to-medium duration (2–4 h): ~½ from carbohydrates
Very long duration (hours+): mostly from fats

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.53%; top: 31.95%; width: 45.73%; height: 68.37%;">

**Glucose cannot be stored freely** — high concentrations disrupt osmotic balance → cell damage.
**Solution:** store as **glycogen**, a *non-osmotic, branched glucose polymer*.
**Structure of Glycogen**
Large, branched polymer (up to **55,000 glucose residues**, ~40 nm).
Mostly **α(1→4)** glycosidic bonds with **α(1→6)** branches every ~12 residues.
Branching → rapid mobilization and solubility.
Found as **granules in cytoplasm**.
Osmotic gradient depends on number of particles → 1 large molecule better than many small
Present in **bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes** (plants store glucose as **starch**).

**Glycogen Breakdown: Phosphorolysis**
**Key enzyme:** *Glycogen phosphorylase* → Cleaves glycogen by **adding orthophosphate (Pi)** instead of water → Produces **glucose-1-phosphate (G1P)**
**Energetic and Cellular Advantages**
Reaction favors breakdown in vivo since **[Pi]/[G1P] ≫ 1**
**No ATP needed** — sugar already phosphorylated
Prevents glucose loss — **G1P cannot exit cell**
**Phosphorolysis > hydrolysis**: conserves energy and maintains cell balance

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 65.02%; top: 33.74%; width: 34.98%; height: 66.26%;">

**Key enzyme:** *Glycogen phosphorylase*
Catalyzes glycogen degradation by **addition of orthophosphate (Pi)**
Product: **Glucose-1-phosphate (G1P)**
This process is called **phosphorolysis** (bond cleavage using Pi)
**Mechanism**
Works at **non-reducing ends** (free –OH on C4)
**Pi** attacks glycosidic bond → cleaves between **C****1**** and O**
**α-configuration** at C1 is retained
**Phosphoglucomutase** converts G1P → **glucose-6-phosphate (G6P)**

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 5.24%; width: 80.35%; height: 93.18%;">

**1.** The signal molecules epinephrine and glucagon bind to specific seven-
transmembrane (7TM) receptors in the plasma membranes of target cells. Epinephrine binds to the b -adrenergic receptor in muscle, whereas glucagon binds to the glucagon receptor in the liver. These binding events activate the G s protein. A specific external signal has been transmitted into the cell through structural changes, first in the receptor and then in the G protein.

**2.** The GTP-bound subunit of G s activates the transmembrane protein adenylate cyclase. This enzyme catalyzes the formation of the second messenger cyclic AMP (cAMP) from ATP.

**3.** The elevated cytoplasmic level of cAMP activates protein kinase A. The binding of cAMP to inhibitory regulatory subunits  triggers their dissociation from the catalytic subunits. The free catalytic subunits are now active.

**4.** Protein kinase A phosphorylates phosphorylase kinase, first on the b subunit and then on the a subunit, which activates glycogen phosphorylase.

**Limits of Glycogen Phosphorylase**
**Phosphorylase** cleaves **α-1,4-glycosidic bonds**
Cannot break **α-1,6 bonds** at branch points
Stops **4 residues away** from a branch
~1 in 12 residues is branched → cleavage halts after ~8 glucose released per branch
**Debranching Enzymes**
Two enzymes allow further breakdown:
**Transferase** → moves a block of 3 residues to another chain
**α-1,6-Glucosidase (debranching enzyme)** → hydrolyzes the branch point
Releases **free glucose**, then phosphorylated by **hexokinase**

Branch removed → glycogen becomes **linear**

**Why Branching Is Needed**
Glycogen synthase makes **only α-1,4 linkages**
Branching enzyme creates **α-1,6 linkages** → produces the branched structure
**How Branching Occurs**
After a linear chain is formed (~≥11 residues)
Branching enzyme **cuts an α-1,4 bond**
Transfers a block of **~7 glucosyl residues**
Reattaches them via an **α-1,6 linkage** at an interior position
New branch point must be **≥4 residues away** from an existing branch
**Why Branching Is Important**
**Increases glycogen solubility**
Creates **many nonreducing ends**
↑ sites for **phosphorylase** (breakdown)
↑ sites for **glycogen synthase** (synthesis)
Result: **much faster synthesis and degradation**

**Reciprocal Control of Glycogen Breakdown & Synthesis**
**Hormonal control (glucagon/epinephrine)**
cAMP → **PKA** activation.
PKA **activates glycogen breakdown** – phosphorylates **phosphorylase kinase → phosphorylase a**
PKA **inhibits glycogen synthesis** – phosphorylates **glycogen synthase → inactive (b form)** → Breakdown ON, synthesis OFF.** **
**Switch to Glycogen Synthesis**
**Protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) actions**
**Dephosphorylates**: – phosphorylase a → b (turns OFF breakdown) – phosphorylase kinase (turns OFF breakdown) – glycogen synthase b → a (turns ON synthesis)
**Regulation of PP1**
PP1 bound to glycogen via **GM (muscle)** or **GL (liver)** subunits.
PKA **inhibits PP1**: – phosphorylates GM → PP1 released from glycogen – phosphorylates PP1 inhibitors → they bind PP1 and block it → Ensures breakdown stays ON during cAMP signaling.

**Insulin: Turning Glycogen Synthesis ON**
**Insulin signaling**
Insulin receptor (RTK) → IRS phosphorylation → kinase cascade.
**Inactivates glycogen synthase kinase**.
PP1 dephosphorylates glycogen synthase → **active a form**.
Insulin also **increases glucose uptake**. → Overall: glycogen stores replenished.
**Key Elements of the Glucose-Sensing Switch**
Glucose shifts phosphorylase a (R→T) to expose Ser14.
PP1 dephosphorylates **phosphorylase** (off) and **glycogen synthase** (on).
PP1 binding to phosphorylase a prevents premature glycogen synthesis.
