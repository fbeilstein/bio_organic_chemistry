Each **motoneuron **that leaves the spinal cord innervates multiple muscle fibers, with the number of fibers innervated depending on the type of muscle. All the muscle fibers innervated by a single nerve fiber are called a **motor unit.** Small muscles that react rapidly and whose control must be exact may have  as few as 2-3 muscle fibers per motor unit (in some of the laryngeal muscles). **Slow Fibers (Type 1, Red Muscle)**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 18.30%; width: 80.35%; height: 74.18%;">

1. Slow fibers are smaller than fast fibers.

2. Slow fibers are also innervated by smaller nerve fibers.

3. Slow fibers have a more extensive blood vessel system and more capillaries to supply extra amounts of oxygen compared with fast fibers,

4. Slow fibers have greatly increased numbers of mitochondria to support high levels of oxidative metabolism.

5. Slow fibers contain large amounts of myoglobin, an iron-containing protein similar to hemoglobin in red blood cells. Myoglobin combines with oxygen
and stores it until needed, which also greatly speeds oxygen transport to the mitochondria. The myoglobin gives the slow muscle a reddish appearance—
hence, the name red muscle.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 19.08%; top: 2.87%; width: 18.86%; height: 11.87%;">

**Myoglobin **

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 13.41%; top: 11.18%; width: 53.56%; height: 80.20%;">

Small, oxygen-binding protein found mainly in muscle tissue.
Stores and releases O₂ to meet high metabolic demands during muscle activity.
Single polypeptide chain (~153 amino acids) with a heme group containing Fe²⁺.
Similar in structure to a subunit of hemoglobin, but monomeric.
Exhibits hyperbolic O₂-binding curve → high oxygen affinity, no cooperativity.
Facilitates oxygen diffusion from blood (hemoglobin) to mitochondria.
Abundant in slow-twitch (red) muscle fibers → enhances endurance.
Myoglobinuria (presence in urine) indicates muscle damage (e.g., rhabdomyolysis).

</div>

</div>

**Fast Fibers (Type II, White Muscle)**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 17.41%; width: 80.35%; height: 77.45%;">

1. Fast fibers are large for great strength of contraction.

2. Fast fibers have an extensive sarcoplasmic reticulum for rapid release of calcium ions to initiate contraction.

3. Large amounts of glycolytic enzymes are present in fast fibers for rapid release of energy by the glycolytic process.

4. Fast fibers have a less extensive blood supply than slow fibers because oxidative metabolism is of secondary importance.

5. Fast fibers have fewer mitochondria than slow fibers, also because oxidative metabolism is secondary. A deficit of red myoglobin in fast muscle gives it the
name white muscle.

The impulse arriving in the end of the motor neuron increases the permeability of its endings to Ca2+, which enters the endings and triggers exocytosis of the acetylcholine (ACh)-containing synaptic vesicles. ACh diffuses and binds to nicotinic cholinergic (NM) receptors in the motor endplate, which increases Na+ and K+ conductance. The resultant influx of Na+ produces the endplate potential. The current sink created by this local potential depolarizes the adjacent muscle membrane to its firing level. Action potentials are generated on either side of the endplate and are conducted away from the endplate in both directions along the muscle fiber and the muscle contracts. ACh is then removed from the synaptic cleft by acetylcholinesterase.

**Quantal Release at the Neuromuscular Junction**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 12.36%; width: 80.35%; height: 83.09%;">

Each nerve impulse releases ~60 synaptic vesicles, each containing ~10,000 ACh molecules.
At rest, small quanta of ACh are released randomly → miniature endplate potentials (MEPPs) ≈ 0.5 mV.
Ca²⁺ ↑ concentration at the endplate → ↑ size of quanta released
Mg²⁺ ↑ concentration at the endplate → ↓ size of quanta released
A nerve impulse triggers massive vesicle release → endplate potential exceeds threshold, causing muscle contraction.
Quantal release also occurs at noradrenergic, glutamatergic, and other synapses.
Disorders affecting this process:
Myasthenia gravis
Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 13.85%; width: 80.35%; height: 86.15%;">

Autoimmune attack on voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels at presynaptic nerve endings → ↓ Ca²⁺ influx → ↓ acetylcholine release

**Malignant Hyperthermia (MH)**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 13.55%; width: 80.35%; height: 83.38%;">

A life-threatening hypermetabolic reaction of skeletal muscle triggered by certain drugs in genetically susceptible individuals.
It’s caused by uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
**Triggers:** Volatile anesthetics(Halothane, Sevoflurane, Desflurane, Isoflurane), Depolarizing muscle relaxant (Succinylcholine - the most classic trigger). These agents abnormally activate the ryanodine receptor (RyR1) or dihydropyridine receptor, leading to massive Ca²⁺ release inside muscle cells.
**Reason:** mutation in RYR1 (ryanodine receptor type 1) or less commonly CACNA1S (DHPR α1 subunit). Result → Ca²⁺ floods the cytosol → sustained contraction → heat, acidosis, and cell damage.
**Treatment: **Immediate administration of dantrolene, a RyR1 antagonist that stops Ca²⁺ release, symptomatic (cool the patient, correct acidosis and electrolyte imbalance).
Incidence ≈ 1:10,000–1:250,000 anesthetic procedures.
Carriers are usually asymptomatic until exposed to triggering agents.

**Single twitch**: A single action potential produces one brief contraction and relaxation.

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.08%; top: 1.38%; width: 37.45%; height: 97.33%;">

**Step 1:** Generation of an action potential in the motor neuron causes the small, saclike vesicles within the terminal axon to release acetylcholine (ACh). ACh diffuses across the synaptic cleft and attaches to specialized ACh receptors on the sarcolemma. Almost perfect symmetry exists between the “imprint” of the presynaptic vesicles that contain ACh and the “imprint” of the postsynaptic receptors that capture ACh.
**Step 2:** The muscle action potential depolarizes the transverse tubules at the sarcomere’s A–I junction. Step 3: Depolarization of the T-tubule system causes Ca2+ release from the lateral sacs (terminal cisternae) of the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
**Step 4:** Ca2+ binds to troponin–tropomyosin in the actin filaments. This releases the inhibition that prevented actin from combining with myosin.
**Step 5:** During muscle action, actin combines with myosin–ATP. Actin also activates the enzyme myosin ATPase, which then splits ATP. The reaction’s energy produces myosin crossbridge movement and creates tension.
**Step 6:** ATP binds to the myosin crossbridge; this breaks the actin–myosin bond and allows the crossbridge to dissociate from actin. The thick and thin filaments then slide past each other and the muscle shortens.
**Step 7:** Crossbridge activation continues when Ca2+ concentration remains high enough (from membrane depolarization) to inhibit the troponin–tropomyosin system.
**Step 8:** When muscle stimulation ceases, intracellular Ca2+ concentration rapidly decreases as Ca2+ moves back into the lateral sacs of the sarcoplasmic reticulum through active transport that requires ATP hydrolysis.
**Step 9:** Ca2+ removal restores the inhibitory action of troponin–tropomyosin. In the presence of ATP, actin and myosin remain in the dissociated, relaxed state.

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.17%; width: 80.35%; height: 92.58%;">

In eukaryotic cells, motor proteins act on structural elements such as actin filaments and microtubules to generate the movements that allow the cells to reorganize their contents, change their shape, and even crawl or swim. For example, muscle contraction is accomplished by the motor protein myosin pulling on actin filaments.

The proteins on which we will focus in this lecture move along actin and microtubules— protein filaments composed of repeating subunits. 

The motor proteins cycle between forms having high or low affinity for the filament tracks in response to ATP binding and hydrolysis, enabling a bind, pull, and release mechanism that generates motion.

ATP Binding → Actin–Myosin Detachment
Tight ATP binding releases myosin from actin, using the free energy of ATP binding.

Search Phase (Actin⁻ State)
Myosin lever arm relaxes from its high-energy “up” position.
The detached head performs a biased Brownian search for a new actin site.
ATP hydrolysis occurs during this phase.

Rebinding and Powerstroke
Upon finding a new site, myosin binds weakly → then strongly.
Tight binding releases ADP·Pi, driving the lever-arm swing (powerstroke) that moves the cargo.

Load Dependence:
- Heavy load: slows ADP release → increases duty ratio → slower movement.
- Light load: faster ADP release → longer steps and faster motion.

The mechanism explains actomyosin energy coupling without invoking large internal deformations, instead relying on thermodynamic partitioning of ATP energy.

Myosins turn over ~1 ATP per second → unusually slow for an enzyme.

18O Labeling Experiment
ATP hydrolysis studied using H₂¹⁸O (water with heavy oxygen).
Product phosphate contained 2–3 oxygens from water, not just 1.
**Conclusion:** ATP hydrolysis is reversible inside the myosin active site.

Enzyme-Bound Equilibrium
Free ATP hydrolysis: K ≈ 1.4 × 10⁵ (strongly favorable).
On enzyme: K ≈ 10 → near equilibrium between ATP and ADP·Pi.
**Reason:** enzymes stabilize the transition state, equalizing reactant/product energies.

Rate-Limiting Step
Hydrolysis itself ≠ rate-limiting.
Product release, especially Pi release, is rate-limiting.

Functional Implication
Myosin often holds ADP + Pi before release.
This persistence allows tight coupling between chemical energy and mechanical movement.

**Efficiency of Muscle Contraction.**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 14.74%; width: 80.35%; height: 78.93%;">

The percentage of the input energy to muscle (the chemical energy in nutrients) that can be converted into work, even under the best conditions, is **less than 25%**, with the remainder becoming heat. 

The reason for this low efficiency is that about one-half of the energy in foodstuffs is lost during the formation of ATP and, even then, only 40% to 45% of the energy in ATP itself can later be converted into work. 

Depends on contraction velocity:
**Slow contraction**: small amounts of maintenance heat are released during contraction, even though little or no work is performed, thereby decreasing the conversion efficiency to as little as zero.
**Moderate velocity (about 30% of maximum): maximal efficiency.**
**Rapid contraction: **much of the energy is used to overcome viscous friction within the muscle itself, and this too reduces the efficiency of contraction.

</div>

Na⁺/K⁺ pumping to maintain membrane potential

Stored ATP ≈ 4 mM → lasts **1–2 s** of maximal contraction

** 1. Phosphocreatine System**
**Phosphocreatine (PCr)** stores high-energy phosphate bonds
Transfers phosphate to ADP → regenerates ATP instantly
Supply lasts **~5–8 s** of maximal effort
Provides **immediate energy** (sprints, power lifts)

**Breakdown of glycogen → pyruvate/lactate + ATP**
Works **without oxygen** (anaerobic)
~2.5× faster ATP formation than oxidative metabolism
Supports activity for **up to ~1 min**, but lactic acid accumulation limits duration
**3. Oxidative Metabolism**
**Oxygen + pyruvate/fatty acids/amino acids → ATP**
Supplies **>95%** of ATP for long-term contraction
Dominates during **aerobic, sustained exercise**
**Fuel sources:**
Short-to-medium duration (2–4 h): ~½ from carbohydrates
Very long duration (hours+): mostly from fats

**Smooth vs. Skeletal Muscle Contraction**
**Contraction Characteristics**
**Smooth muscle:** slow, tonic contractions lasting seconds to days.
**Skeletal muscle:** rapid, phasic contractions.
**Cross-Bridge Cycling**
Much **slower** in smooth muscle (1/10–1/300 of skeletal).
**Low ATPase activity →** slower ATP hydrolysis and movement.
**Energy Use**
Requires only **1/10–1/300** as much energy to sustain tension.
Enables **long-term contraction** in viscera with minimal energy.

**Contraction Dynamics**
**Slow onset:** 50–100 ms after excitation.
**Total contraction time:** 1–3 s (vs. ~0.1 s in skeletal).
**Force**
Despite fewer myosin filaments, **greater max force** (4–6 kg/cm² vs. 3–4 kg/cm²).
Due to prolonged actin–myosin attachment.
**Latch Mechanism**
Maintains force with **minimal energy** and **reduced excitation** — ideal for tonic contraction.
**Stress–Relaxation Response**
After stretch, tension **returns to baseline** within 15–60 s.
Allows hollow organs (e.g. bladder) to **maintain pressure** despite volume changes.

In place of troponin, smooth muscle cells contain a large amount of calmodulin. Calmodulin initiates contraction by activating the myosin cross bridges. 
**1.** The calcium concentration in the cytosolic fluid increases as a result of the influx through calcium channels and/or release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
**2.** The calcium ions bind reversibly with calmodulin.
**3.** The calmodulin-calcium complex then joins with and activates myosin light chain kinase, a phosphorylating enzyme.
**4.** One of the light chains of each myosin head, called the regulatory chain, becomes phosphorylated in response to this myosin kinase and obtains the capability of binding repetitively with the actin filament and proceeding through the entire cycling process of intermittent pulls, the same as what occurs for skeletal muscle, thus causing muscle contraction.

**Source of Ca²⁺ in Smooth Muscle**
Smooth muscle contraction **depends mainly on extracellular Ca²⁺ ** → sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) is **poorly developed**.
Ca²⁺ enters **through membrane channels** during an **action potential or stimulus**.
[Ca²⁺] outside ≈ 10⁻³ M, inside ≈ 10⁻⁷ M → strong diffusion gradient.
**Latency before contraction:** 200–300 ms → ~50× slower than skeletal muscle.
**Role of SR and Caveolae**
SR forms a **rudimentary network** near the membrane.
**Caveolae** = small membrane invaginations acting as analogs of **T-tubules**.
Action potentials in caveolae trigger **Ca²⁺ release** from adjacent SR.
More developed SR → **faster contraction**.
**Smooth muscle force strongly depends** on extracellular Ca²⁺ concentration. → Contraction ceases if [Ca²⁺]ₑ falls below 1/3–1/10 normal.

Allows **prolonged tension with minimal energy use**.
When **myosin kinase** and **phosphatase** are both active → fast cycling.
When activity decreases → heads stay **attached longer**, fewer ATP cycles.
Result: → **Force maintained (latched)** → **Energy conserved (low ATP use)**

**Key causes of long plateau:**
**Two channel types:**
**Fast Na⁺ channels:** open briefly → initial spike
**Slow L-type Ca²⁺/Na⁺ channels:** open longer → sustain depolarization
**↓ K⁺ permeability** → less positive charge leaves cell → maintains plateau
When Ca²⁺/Na⁺ channels close → ↑ K⁺ efflux → **rapid repolarization**

**Excitation–Contraction Coupling in Cardiac Muscle**
**Pathway:**
AP spreads along **sarcolemma** and **T-tubules**
Triggers **Ca²⁺ release** from **sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)**
Ca²⁺ binds **troponin → actin–myosin sliding → contraction**
**Dual Source of Calcium**
Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac contraction depends on:
**Ca²⁺ from SR** (via ryanodine receptors)
**Ca²⁺ influx from T-tubules** (via L-type voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels)
Ca²⁺ entering through T-tubules **triggers further Ca²⁺ release** from SR (Ca²⁺-induced Ca²⁺ release)
**Without T-tubule Ca²⁺ influx → weak contraction** (SR stores are limited)

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 55.47%; top: 62.81%; width: 43.73%; height: 36.59%;">

**Cardiac T-tubules:**
5× wider → **25× volume** of skeletal T-tubules
Contain negatively charged **mucopolysaccharides** that **bind Ca²⁺**
**Extracellular Ca²⁺ critical:**
Heart stops beating in **Ca²⁺-free medium**
Skeletal muscle contraction **not affected** by moderate Ca²⁺ changes

**SA and AV nodes **= modified **cardiomyocytes (not neurons!)**
**Sinus (SA) node** = pacemaker of the heart
**Unique property:** *Self-excitation → spontaneous action potentials*
**Resting potential:** about **–55 to –60 mV** (less negative than –90 mV in ventricles) → **Fast Na⁺ channels inactivated at -55 mV** → only **slow Ca²⁺–Na⁺ channels** remain functional
**How the SA Node Fires Automatically**
**Leakiness to Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ (“funny current”, If):**
Positive ions **slowly enter** the cell between beats
Membrane potential **drifts upward** (slow depolarization)
**Threshold (~–40 mV):**
**L-type Ca²⁺ channels open** → rapid depolarization → **action potential**
**Repolarization:**
Ca²⁺ channels **inactivate**
**K⁺ channels open** → K⁺ **exits**, returning cell to negative potential
**Reset and Next Beat**
**Hyperpolarization:**
After AP, K⁺ channels stay open briefly → potential ~–60 mV
Then K⁺ channels **close gradually**, Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ leak in again
Membrane slowly depolarizes → **reaches threshold → new AP**
This **spontaneous cycle repeats continuously** → rhythmic heartbeat

**Delays conduction** (~0.13–0.16 s total) → Ensures atria finish contracting **before** ventricles begin.
**Enforces one-way conduction** → Prevents backflow of impulses from ventricles to atria.
**Regulates timing** between atrial and ventricular systole.
**Why the AV Node Delays Signal**
**Fewer gap junctions** → High resistance between cells → Slow impulse spread.
**Smaller fiber diameter** → Slower conduction velocity.
**Low resting potential** → Slower depolarization (mostly via Ca²⁺ channels). *(Unlike Purkinje fibers that use fast Na⁺ channels.)*
**Result:** ~0.09 s delay inside AV node + ~0.04 s in AV bundle.
**Why the AV Node Is One-Way**
Only the **AV bundle** crosses the **fibrous barrier** between atria and ventricles.
Action potentials can go **downward** but not back **upward** (electrophysiological block).
Prevents **re-entry circuits** → protects from arrhythmias.
