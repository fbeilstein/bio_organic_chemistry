The human genome encodes about 800 functional GPCRs (4%) of the identified human proteins. 
detect and respond to many different types of signals, including odorants, tastants, neurotransmitters, hormones involved in glycogen and fat metabolism, growth factors, and even physical stimuli (photons of light). 
approximately 35% of all drugs used in humans are agonists or antagonists of GPCRs
at least 134 GPCRs are targets of drugs approved in the US and EU.

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 5.83%; width: 80.35%; height: 87.13%;">

The heterotrimeric guanine nucleotide—binding proteins (G proteins) are signal transducers that communicate signals from many hormones, neurotransmitters, chemokines, and autocrine and paracrine factors. The extracellular signals are received by members of a large superfamily of receptors with seven membrane-spanning regions that activate the G proteins, which route the signals to several distinct intracellular signaling pathways. These pathways interact with one another to form a network that regulates metabolic enzymes, ion channels, transporters, and other components of the cellular machinery controlling a broad range of cellular processes, including transcription, motility, contractility, and secretion. These cellular processes in turn regulate systemic functions such as embryonic development, gonadal development, learning and memory, and organismal homeostasis

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 14.56%;">

**All GPCR signal transduction pathways share the following common elements:**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 21.01%; width: 80.35%; height: 71.29%;">

a membrane-imbedded receptor that contains seven membrane-spanning α helices;
a heterotrimeric G protein, which functions as a receptor-activated switch by
cycling between an active GTP-bound and an inactive GDP-bound state;
A membrane-bound effector protein; and 
proteins that participate in amplification and desensitization of the signaling pathway. Protein kinases and second messengers such as cAMP and Ca2+ often participate in GPCR-mediated signal transduction pathways.

These pathways usually have short-term effects in the cell by quickly modifying the activities of existing proteins, including enzymes and ion channels. Thus these pathways allow cells to respond rapidly to a variety of signals, whether they be physical stimuli such as light or hormonal stimuli such as epinephrine.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.37%; top: 3.66%; width: 80.35%; height: 21.97%;">

All G protein–coupled receptors have the same orientation in the membrane and
contain seven transmembrane α-helical regions (H1–H7), four extracellular
segments, and four cytosolic segments

</div>

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_38_img_50.png) {left=44.62 top=26.01 width=53.10 height=47.98}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.37%; top: 27.61%; width: 26.13%; height: 69.64%;">

The human GPCRs are divided into several families. Members of each family are
especially similar to one another in amino acid sequence and structure. The
families differ from each other in the details of how ligands bind the receptors.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_40_img_52.png) {left=15.96 top=0.00 width=55.45 height=100.00}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 43.50%; top: 50.00%; width: 56.50%; height: 47.58%;">

Family A is the largest family in mammals, segments of several membrane-embedded α helices and extracellular loops form the ligand-binding site. That site is exposed to the exoplasmic surface, but can extend down toward the middle of the membrane bilayer. 

Example: the β-adrenergic receptor, which bind hormones such as epinephrine and norepinephrine. As an example, in binding of epinephrine (adrenaline) to the β-adrenergic receptor, side chains of 15 amino acids located in four transmembrane α helices and the extracellular loop E2 make noncovalent bonds with the bound ligand.

</div>

</div>

---

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.51%; top: 62.05%; width: 82.49%; height: 37.95%;">

The glucagon receptor is a member of family B of the GPCRs. Its ligand glucagon is a 29-amino-acid peptide hormone secreted by the α cells of the pancreatic islets; as we will see later in this chapter, it acts on the liver to trigger glycogen breakdown and secretion of glucose into the bloodstream. While the glucagon receptor has the seven transmembrane α helices characteristic of GPCRs, it also has a large exoplasmic domain that is connected to the first transmembrane α helix. This exoplasmic domain binds tightly to the C-terminus of glucagon, positioning glucagon’s N-terminus to bind to the pocket formed, as in β-adrenergic and other GPCRs, by residues from several transmembrane helices

</div>

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_41_img_53.png) {left=31.44 top=0.00 width=51.86 height=62.05}

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 0.00%; width: 37.37%; height: 41.80%;">

The glutamate receptor is an excitatory neurotransmitter receptor in family C of GPCRs. The long extracellular segment forms a sort of venus flytrap domain that clamps around and binds the glutamate ligand. Many members of this class of GPCRs dimerize through their venus flytrap domains.

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_43_img_56.png) {left=33.94 top=0.00 width=50.00 height=49.32}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.88%; top: 51.38%; width: 82.12%; height: 46.86%;">

When unbound by ligand and in the inactive conformation (R), the receptor has no surface that is complementary to the G protein and thus cannot bind to it. The binding of the agonist induces major conformational changes and enables the receptor to bind to the subunit of the intact heterotrimeric G protein containing GDP.
Binding of the GPCR to the alpha subunit of G induces a change in the conformation of the GDP-binding site on Galpha, allowing a lobe of the protein to
move and thus release the GDP. Next because of its higher concentration in the
cytosol, GTP rapidly binds to the empty guanine nucleotide site in the subunit.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 16.51%;">

**GTP-Binding Proteins Are Frequently Used in Signal Transduction Pathways as On/Off Switches**

</div>

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_44_img_57.png) {left=54.74 top=20.32 width=45.26 height=73.76}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.10%; top: 19.69%; width: 39.64%; height: 80.31%;">

Heterotrimeric G proteins directly bind to and are activated by GPCRs that function as guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs): trigger release of GDP, the G protein then
spontaneously binds GTP, favored by the high intracellular concentration
of GTP relative to that of GDP that induces conformational change involving  two highly conserved segments switch I and switch II, that allow the protein to bind to and activate downstream signaling proteins.

</div>

</div>

---

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 6.97%;">

**Deactivation**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.36%; top: 10.78%; width: 82.06%; height: 84.16%;">

After a brief time, the active G protein is shut off, converting back to the inactive form. It has intrinsic GTPase activity and slowly hydrolyzes the bound GTP to GDP (seconds to minutes) and, thereby altering the conformation of the switch I and switch II segments so that they are unable to bind to the target protein.

The rate of GTP hydrolysis determines the length of time the GTP-binding protein remains in the active conformation and is able to signal its downstream target proteins

Monomeric (often called low-molecular-weight) G proteins, including Ras and various Ras-like proteins such as Ran and Sar, do not directly bind to receptors but act as intermediate proteins in signal transduction pathways. Monomeric G proteins play crucial roles in many pathways that regulate cell division and cell motility. For this reason, mutations in genes encoding these G proteins frequently lead to cancer.

</div>

</div>

---

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_46_img_58.png) {left=24.50 top=0.00 width=67.92 height=46.67}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.88%; top: 49.72%; width: 80.94%; height: 48.52%;">

The rate of GTP hydrolysis  is often modulated by other proteins. For instance, both GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) and regulators of G protein signaling (RGSs) accelerate GTP hydrolysis. 
Many regulators of G protein activity are themselves controlled by extracellular signals.
Toxin of Vibrio cholerae penetrates the plasma membrane of cells, such as those that line the small intestine, and catalyzes a chemical modification of Galpha preventing hydrolysis of GTP. Constant hyperactivation leads to the loss of electrolytes and water from these intestinal cells into the intestinal lumen

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 20.32%; width: 80.35%; height: 73.96%;">

All effector proteins in GPCR signal transduction pathways are either membrane-bound ion channels or membrane-bound enzymes that catalyze the formation of one or more of the second messengers.
Humans have 21 different alpha subunits, 6 beta subunits, and 12 gamma subunits.
The 21 alpha subunits are encoded by 16 genes, several of which undergo alternative splicing. So far as is known, the different beta-gamma subunits are interchangeable, while the different alpha subunits provide specificity to the various G proteins.
GPCRs represent the largest protein family in the human genome- approximately 800 genese.
400 GPCR genes are thought to encode sensory receptors; of these, the majority are in the olfactory system and bind odorants.

</div>

Regulation of adenylyl cyclase activity, which occurs in many cell types, provides fine-tuned control of the cAMP level and thus of the downstream cellular response.
Virtually all of the diverse effects of cAMP are mediated through its activation of protein kinase A (PKA). This protein kinase phosphorylates multiple intracellular target proteins expressed in different cell types.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 0.00%; width: 45.24%; height: 98.72%;">

Inactive PKA is a tetramer consisting of two regulatory (R) subunits and two catalytic (C) subunits. Each of the two R subunits in an R2 dimer has two cAMP-binding domains, called CNB-A and CNB-B, as well as a linker domain of unknown structure. At the C-terminus there is a dimerization domain that binds an AKAP protein. A segment of the CNB-A domain of the R subunit is a pseudosubstrate; its sequence resembles that of a peptide substrate and binds to the active site in the catalytic domain but is not phosphorylated. In the absence of cAMP the pseudosubstrate segment binds to this catalytic site and inhibits its activity.

Binding of cAMP to the CNB-A domain causes a change in its conformation so that it can no longer bind to and inhibit the catalytic subunit; this releases the C subunit, instantly activating its kinase activity.

</div>

**Example: Regulation of glycogen metabolism by cAMP and PKA**

</div>

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_53_img_68.png) {left=51.49 top=14.14 width=48.51 height=42.63}

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_53_img_69.png) {left=51.49 top=58.09 width=48.51 height=41.91}

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_53_img_70.png) {left=16.32 top=7.32 width=33.76 height=43.96}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.91%; top: 52.72%; width: 36.58%; height: 47.28%;">

**(a)** An increase in cytosolic cAMP activates PKA, which phosphorylates glycogen synthase (GS), inhibiting its catalytic activity to synthesize glycogen. Active PKA also promotes glycogen degradation via a protein kinase cascade that activates glycogen phosphorylase. At high cAMP concentrations, PKA also phosphorylates an inhibitor of phosphoprotein phosphatase (PP). Binding of thephosphorylated inhibitor to PP prevents this phosphatase from de-phosphorylating the
activated enzymes in the glycogen phosphorylase kinase cascade or the inactive glycogen synthase. 
**(b)** A decrease in cAMP inactivates PKA, leading to release of the active form of PP. The activation of PP promotes glycogen synthesis and inhibits glycogen degradation.

</div>

**Signal Amplification Occurs in the cAMP-PKA Glycogen Degradation Pathway**

</div>

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_54_img_71.png) {left=50.00 top=17.11 width=50.00 height=69.19}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.25%; top: 15.43%; width: 33.75%; height: 81.50%;">

Amplification up to 108 can be achieved.

PKA always phosphorylates a serine or threonine residue that is located within the common sequence motif: X-Arg-(Arg/Lys)-X-(<u>Ser/Thr</u>)-Φ, where X denotes any amino acid and Φ denotes a hydrophobic amino acid. The Ser or Thr whose side chain is phosphorylated is underlined. Thus many potential protein targets of PKA can be identified simply by analyzing their amino acid sequence.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_55_img_72.png) {left=64.47 top=2.96 width=35.53 height=94.07}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.92%; top: 0.79%; width: 45.57%; height: 97.33%;">

**CREB Links cAMP and PKA to**
**Activation of Gene Transcription**

Following the elevation of cAMP levels and the release of active PKA catalytic subunits, some of the catalytic subunits move into the nucleus. There they phosphorylate serine-133 on a transcription factor called CRE-binding (CREB) protein. CRE = AMP-response element, which
contains the highly conserved nucleotide sequence, 5’-TGACGTCA-3’. 

Phosphorylated CREB binds to CRE-containing target genes and also binds to the co-activator termed CBP/P300. CBP/P300 links CREB to RNA polymerase II and other gene regulatory proteins, thereby stimulating gene transcription.

</div>

</div>

**Anchoring Proteins Localize Effects of cAMP to Specific Regions of the Cell**

</div>

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_56_img_73.png) {left=19.19 top=14.08 width=69.27 height=46.11}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.58%; top: 62.22%; width: 82.42%; height: 37.78%;">

Each of the roughly 50 anchoring proteins, referred to as A kinase–associated proteins (AKAPs), has a two-domain structure: one domain confers a specific subcellular location by binding to a protein found only in a specific subcompartment, and the other binds to the regulatory (R) subunit of PKA. AKAPs regulate cAMP and PKA signaling within the cell both spatially and temporally. One AKAP in heart muscle anchors PKA to the outer nuclear membrane. From this location, the released, catalytically active C subunits readily enter the nucleus, where they phosphorylate and activate the CREB transcription factor

</div>

**Example: Ca****2+**** activates calmodulin**

</div>

![](./lecture_08_molecular_signaling/images/slide_59_img_77.png) {left=60.25 top=14.74 width=39.05 height=79.33}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.08%; top: 18.80%; width: 41.23%; height: 71.22%;">

Calmodulin is a widely distributed cytosolic protein that contains four
-binding sites, one in each of its EF hand (helix-loop-helix) motifs. At
Cytosolic concentrations about 5x10-7 M, binding of Ca2+ to calmodulin changes the protein’s conformation
to one in which hydrophobic side chains become more exposed to solvent. The resulting Ca/calmodulin complex can wrap around exposed helices with specialized sequences in
various target proteins, thereby altering their activities.

</div>
