1. During the normal synthesis and degradation of cellular proteins, some amino acids that are released from protein breakdown and are not needed for new protein synthesis undergo oxidative degradation.

2. When a diet is rich in protein and the ingested amino acids exceed the body’s needs for protein synthesis, the surplus is catabolized; **amino acids cannot be stored**.

3. During starvation or in uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, when carbohydrates are either unavailable or not properly utilized, cellular proteins are used as fuel.

4. Under all these metabolic conditions, amino acids lose their amino groups to
form α-keto acids, the “carbon skeletons” of amino acids. The α-keto acids
undergo oxidation to CO2 and H2O or, often more importantly, provide three-
and four-carbon units that can be converted by gluconeogenesis into glucose,
the fuel for brain, skeletal muscle, and other tissues.

5. The pathways of amino acid catabolism are quite similar in most organisms.

**Amino acid catabolism in mammals**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 15.92%; width: 80.35%; height: 80.71%;">

Four amino acids play central roles in nitrogen metabolism: glutamate, glutamine, alanine, and aspartate. 
These are the ones most easily converted into citric acid cycle intermediates: 
glutamate and glutamine to α-ketoglutarate, 
alanine to pyruvate, and 
aspartate to oxaloacetate. 
Glutamate and glutamine are especially important, acting as a kind of general collection point for amino groups. In the cytosol of liver cells (hepatocytes), amino groups from most amino acids are transferred to α-ketoglutarate to form glutamate, which enters mitochondria and gives up its amino group to form NH4+. 
Excess ammonia generated in most other tissues is converted to the amide nitrogen of glutamine, which passes to the liver, then into liver mitochondria.
Glutamine or glutamate or both are present in higher concentrations than other amino acids in most tissues.
In skeletal muscle, excess amino groups are generally transferred to pyruvate to form alanine.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: -0.00%; width: 39.40%; height: 100.00%;">

The **liver is the only organ** that fully performs the urea cycle.
The cycle converts **toxic ammonia (NH₃)**, produced during amino acid breakdown, into **urea**, which is safe and excreted by the kidneys.
Two urea-cycle enzymes are located in the **mitochondria of hepatocytes**, the other three in the cytosol — so the liver has the necessary compartmentalization.
When other tissues generate ammonia, they send it to the liver in the form of **alanine** or **glutamine**, and the liver detoxifies it through the urea cycle.

**Enzyme-catalyzed transaminations.**

The first step in the catabolism of most L-amino acids, once they have reached the **liver**, is removal of the α-amino groups, promoted by enzymes called aminotransferases or transaminases.

In many aminotransferase reactions, α-ketoglutarate is the amino group acceptor. All aminotransferases have pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) as cofactor. Although the reaction is shown here in the direction of transfer of the amino group to α-
ketoglutarate, it is readily reversible (ΔG′ ≈ 0 kJ/mol).

</div>

All aminotransferases have the same prosthetic group and the same reaction mechanism. The prosthetic group is pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), the coenzyme form of pyridoxine, or vitamin B6.

Pyridoxal phosphate functions as an intermediate carrier of amino groups at the active site of aminotransferases. It undergoes reversible transformations between its aldehyde form, pyridoxal phosphate, which can accept an amino
group, and its aminated form, pyridoxamine phosphate, which can donate its amino group to an α-keto acid.

**Glucose-alanine cycle.** 

Alanine serves as a carrier of ammonia and of the carbon skeleton of pyruvate from skeletal muscle to liver. The ammonia is excreted and the pyruvate is used to produce glucose, which is returned to the muscle.

**Reaction catalyzed by glutamate dehydrogenase.** 

The glutamate dehydrogenase of mammalian liver has the unusual capacity to use either NAD+ or NADP+ as cofactor. The glutamate dehydrogenases of plants
and microorganisms are generally specific for one or the other. The mammalian enzyme is allosterically regulated by GTP and ADP.

**Ammonia transport in the form of glutamine. **

Excess ammonia in tissues is added to glutamate to form glutamine, a process catalyzed by **glutamine synthetase**. After transport in the bloodstream, the glutamine enters the liver, and
NH4+ is liberated in mitochondria by the enzyme **glutaminase**.

In metabolic acidosis there is an increase in glutamine processing by the kidneys. Not all the excess NH4+ thus produced is released into the bloodstream or converted to urea; some is excreted directly into the urine. In the kidney, the NH4+ forms salts with metabolic acids, facilitating their removal in the urine. Bicarbonate produced by the decarboxylation of α-ketoglutarate in the citric acid cycle can also serve as a buffer in blood plasma. Taken together, these effects tend to counteract acidosis.

</div>

**Ammonia Is Toxic to Animals**
**Why ammonia is dangerous**
Ammonia is highly toxic, especially to the **brain**.
Symptoms: cognitive impairment, ataxia, seizures, brain swelling → death.
In blood, **98% of ammonia is NH₄⁺**, which **cannot cross membranes**.
The small amount of **NH₃** *can cross all membranes*, including the blood–brain barrier → accumulates inside cells as NH₄⁺.
**Detoxification pathways**
**Astrocytes** (only) express **glutamine synthetase**, converting:
α-ketoglutarate → glutamate → glutamine
Consequences:
Depletes **glutamate** (key neurotransmitter).
May reduce GABA levels.
But this pathway is **insufficient in ammonia overload**.
Multiple metabolic pathways send excess ammonia to the **mitochondria of hepatocytes**, where it is converted to **urea**.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 59.81%; top: 2.28%; width: 40.34%; height: 93.10%;">

**NH****3**** Disrupts the Brain**
**1. Potassium homeostasis fails**
NH₄⁺ competes with **K⁺** for **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** transport.
→ ↑ extracellular K⁺.
**2. Neurons become hyperexcitable**
Excess K⁺ enters neurons via **NKCC1** (Na⁺–K⁺–2Cl⁻ cotransporter), bringing Cl⁻ with it.
High intracellular Cl⁻ reverses neuronal response to **GABA** → **GABA becomes depolarizing instead of inhibitory**.
Result: **abnormal firing → ataxia + seizures**.
**3. Brain swelling (edema)**
Persistent NH₄⁺ → astrocyte channel dysfunction → water influx → **astrocyte swelling → fatal cerebral edema**.

</div>

</div>

**Step 1** – Ornithine Transcarbamoylase (mitochondria)
Carbamoyl phosphate + ornithine → citrulline + Pi
Citrulline is then transported to the cytosol.

**Step 2** – Argininosuccinate Synthetase (cytosol)
Citrulline + aspartate + ATP → argininosuccinate
Forms a citrullyl-AMP intermediate; introduces the second amino group.

**Step 3** – Argininosuccinase (cytosol)
Argininosuccinate → arginine + fumarate
Only reversible step; fumarate returns to TCA (→ malate).

**Step 4** – Arginase (cytosol)
Arginine → urea + ornithine
Ornithine re-enters mitochondria to start the next cycle.

**Aminotransferases (ALT, AST)**
**ALT (Alanine aminotransferase, GPT)**
**AST (Aspartate aminotransferase, GOT)** **↑ in blood → liver or heart cell injury.** Leak into serum after **heart attack**, **viral/drug-induced hepatitis**, or **toxin exposure** (carbon tetrachloride, chloroform).
**Timing after myocardial infarction (MI):**
AST rises early after MI.
ALT rises later (more liver-specific).
**Creatine Kinase (CK or CPK)**
First enzyme to rise after MI.
Appears quickly and also **disappears quickly**, useful for early diagnosis.
CK-MB isoform is most specific for cardiac injury.
**Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH)**
Elevated in **heart, liver, or muscle damage**.
Rises later than CK and remains high longer.
LDH-1 > LDH-2 (“flip”) suggests **MI**.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 61.65%; top: 1.68%; width: 38.50%; height: 96.83%;">

**Medical Relevance**
**Why these enzymes rise**
Cell injury → loss of membrane integrity → cytosolic enzymes leak into blood.
Measuring specific patterns **helps identify the organ damaged and timing of injury**.
**Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)**
**↑ in cholestasis**, bile duct obstruction, bone disorders (high osteoblast activity).
**Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (GGT)**
Sensitive marker of **bile duct injury** and **alcohol abuse**.
↑ GGT + ↑ ALP → hepatobiliary disease.
**Amylase & Lipase**
**Amylase**: rises quickly in **acute pancreatitis**.
**Lipase**: more specific for pancreas; stays elevated longer.
