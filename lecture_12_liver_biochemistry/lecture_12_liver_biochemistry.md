**Apolipoproteins & Lipoproteins: Transport of Dietary and Endogenous Lipids**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.24%; top: 24.53%; width: 74.79%; height: 73.89%;">

**Apolipoproteins**
- Lipid-binding proteins that transport TAGs, phospholipids, cholesterol, and cholesteryl esters.
- “Apo-” = lipid-free protein.
- Combine with lipids to form **lipoproteins**:
  - Hydrophobic core: TAGs, cholesteryl esters  
  - Hydrophilic shell: phospholipids + apolipoproteins

- Key roles:
  - Structural support (apoB-48, apoB-100)
  - Enzyme cofactors (apoC-II activates lipoprotein lipase)
  - Receptor ligands (apoE for liver uptake)
  - Transfer protein interactions (apoA-I activates LCAT)

</div>

</div>

---

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 18.92%; top: 7.51%; width: 82.07%; height: 84.99%;">

**Classes of Lipoproteins (by density)**

**1. Chylomicrons (least dense, largest)**
- **Origin:** intestinal mucosa  
- **Apos:** apoB-48, apoC-II, apoE  
- **Function:** transport **dietary** TAGs to muscle & adipose  
- **Key process:** lipoprotein lipase (activated by apoC-II) removes TAGs → chylomicron remnants  
- **Remnants:** taken up by liver via apoE receptors  
- **Rich in:** triglycerides

**2. VLDL – Very-Low-Density Lipoprotein**
- **Origin:** liver  
- **Apos:** apoB-100, apoC-II, apoE  
- **Function:** deliver endogenous TAGs to tissues  
- **Becomes:** IDL after partial TAG removal  
- **Rich in:** triglycerides (but less than chylomicrons)

</div>

</div>

---

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 19.58%; top: 3.71%; width: 57.93%; height: 92.59%;">

**3. IDL – Intermediate-Density Lipoprotein**
- **Origin**: VLDL catabolism  
- **Apos**: apoB-100 + apoE  
- **Fates**:  
  - 50% → taken up by liver via apoE  
  - 50% → converted by hepatic lipase to LDL  
- **Rich in**: TAGs + cholesterol  
- **Importance**: transient but atherogenic if elevated

**4. LDL – Low-Density Lipoprotein**
- **Origin**: IDL  
- **Apos**: apoB-100 (only)  
- **Function**: deliver cholesterol to peripheral tissues  
- **Receptor**: LDL receptor (binds apoB-100)  
- **Clinical**: “Bad cholesterol”; atherosclerosis risk  
- **Rich in**: cholesterol

**5. HDL – High-Density Lipoprotein**
- **Origin**: liver and intestine  
- **Apos**: apoA-I, apoA-II  
- **Function**: reverse cholesterol transport → to liver  
- **Activates**: LCAT via apoA-I  
- **Clinical**: “Good cholesterol”; anti-atherogenic  
- **Rich in**: protein

**Mobilization of triacylglycerols stored in adipose tissue.**
When low levels of glucose in the blood trigger the release of **glucagon**, 
1. the hormone binds its receptor in the adipocyte membrane and thus 
2. stimulates adenylyl cyclase, via a G protein, to produce cAMP. This activates PKA, which phosphorylates 
3. the hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) and
4. perilipin molecules on the surface of the lipid droplet. Phosphorylation of perilipin causes 
5. dissociation of the protein CGI-58 from perilipin. CGI-58 then recruits adipose triacylglycerol lipase (ATGL) to the droplet surface and stimulates its lipase activity. 
6. Active ATGL converts triacylglycerols to diacylglycerols. The phosphorylated perilipin associates with phosphorylated HSL, allowing it access to the surface of the lipid droplet, where 
7. it converts diacylglycerols to monoacylglycerols. 
8. A third lipase, monoacylglycerol lipase (MGL), hydrolyzes monoacylglycerols.
9 Fatty acids leave the adipocyte, and are transported in the blood bound to serum albumin. They are released from the albumin and 
10. enter a myocyte via a specific fatty acid transporter. 
11. In the myocyte, fatty acids are oxidized to CO2, and the energy of oxidation is conserved in ATP, which fuels muscle contraction and other energy-requiring metabolism in the myocyte.

**Fatty Acids Are Activated and Transported into Mitochondria**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 20.32%; width: 80.35%; height: 61.80%;">

The enzymes of fatty acid oxidation in animal cells are located in the mitochondrial matrix (1948, Kennedy and Lehninger).
The fatty acids with chain lengths of 12 or fewer carbons enter mitochondria without the help of membrane transporters.
14 or more carbons (majority of the FFAs) -** carnitine shuttle -> 3 reactions**
The carnitine-mediated entry process is the rate-limiting step for oxidation of fatty acids in mitochondria and a control point.

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](images/slide_22_img_28.png) {left=36.11 top=9.60 width=63.89 height=44.31}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 63.10%; height: 7.96%;">

**Second reaction of the carnitine shuttle**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.91%; top: 51.20%; width: 83.09%; height: 48.80%;">

Fatty acids destined for mitochondrial oxidation are transiently attached to the hydroxyl group of carnitine to form fatty acyl–carnitine, catalyzed by carnitine acyltransferase 1 (also called carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1, CPT1). The acyl-CoA is converted to the carnitine ester as it passes through the outer membrane. The fatty acyl–carnitine ester then diffuses across the intermembrane space and enters the matrix by passive transport through the  acyl-carnitine/carnitine cotransporter of the inner mitochondrial membrane. This cotransporter moves one molecule of carnitine from the matrix to the intermembrane space as one molecule of fatty acyl–carnitine moves into the matrix.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.91%; top: 17.41%; width: 20.20%; height: 36.51%;">

Fatty acyl–CoA esters formed on the cytosolic side of the outer mitochondrial membrane:
can be transported into the mitochondrion and oxidized to produce ATP
can be used in the cytosol to synthesize membrane lipids.

**Third reaction of the carnitine shuttle**

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 9.40%; width: 80.35%; height: 84.28%;">

■ The fatty acids of triacylglycerols furnish a large fraction of the oxidative energy in animals. Dietary triacylglycerols are emulsified in the small intestine by bile salts, hydrolyzed by intestinal lipases, absorbed by intestinal epithelial cells, reconverted into triacylglycerols, then formed into chylomicrons by combination with specific apolipoproteins.

■ Chylomicrons deliver triacylglycerols to tissues, where lipoprotein lipase releases free fatty acids for entry into cells. Triacylglycerols stored in adipose tissue are mobilized by a hormone-sensitive triacylglycerol lipase. The released fatty acids bind to serum albumin and are carried in the blood to the heart, skeletal muscle, and other tissues that use fatty acids for fuel.

■ Once inside cells, fatty acids are activated at the outer mitochondrial membrane by conversion to fatty acyl–CoA thioesters. Fatty acyl–CoA that is to be oxidized enters mitochondria in three steps, via the carnitine shuttle.

</div>

**Methylmalonic Acid (MMA) — Clinical Use**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 14.44%; width: 80.35%; height: 81.01%;">

• **Why measure MMA?**
  – Serum B₁₂ levels are unreliable (inactive forms inflate results).
  – MMA rises only when B₁₂-dependent methylmalonyl-CoA mutase fails.
  – Best marker of functional B₁₂ deficiency.

• **When is it useful?**
  – Borderline B₁₂ levels (200–400 pg/mL)
  – Unexplained neuropathy or cognitive decline
  – Elderly patients with normal B₁₂ but symptoms
  – Differentiating B₁₂ vs folate deficiency
      ▸ B₁₂ deficiency: ↑ MMA + ↑ homocysteine  
      ▸ Folate deficiency: normal MMA, ↑ homocysteine

• **Special notes**
  – Elevated MMA is early and sensitive.
  – False positives in renal failure.
  – Used in newborn screening for metabolic disorders.

**Comparison of β oxidation in mitochondria and in peroxisomes and glyoxysomes.** 
The peroxisomal/glyoxysomal system differs from the mitochondrial system in three respects: **(1)** the peroxisomal system prefers very-long-chain fatty acids; **(2)** in the first oxidative step, electrons pass directly to O2, generating H2O2; and **(3)** the NADH formed in the second oxidative step cannot be reoxidized in the peroxisome or glyoxysome, so reducing equivalents are exported to the cytosol, eventually entering mitochondria. When the diet provides a ready source of carbohydrate as fuel, β oxidation of fatty acids is unnecessary and is therefore down-regulated. Two enzymes are key to the coordination of fatty acid metabolism: acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC), the first enzyme in the synthesis of fatty acids, and carnitine acyltransferase **1**, which limits the transport of fatty acids into the mitochondrial matrix for β oxidation. Ingestion of a high-carbohydrate meal raises the blood glucose level and thus **1** triggers the release of insulin. **2** Insulin-dependent protein phosphatase dephosphorylates ACC, activating it. **3** ACC catalyzes the formation of malonyl-CoA (the first intermediate of fatty acid synthesis), and **4** malonyl-CoA inhibits carnitine acyltransferase **1**, thereby preventing fatty acid entry into the mitochondrial matrix. When [AMP] rises, AMPK also phosphorylates and inactivates ACC. The concentration of malonyl-CoA falls, the inhibition of fatty acid entry into mitochondria is relieved, and fatty acids **7** enter the mitochondrial matrix and **8** become the major fuel. **Amino acid catabolism in mammals**

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 15.92%; width: 80.35%; height: 80.71%;">

Four amino acids play central roles in nitrogen metabolism: glutamate, glutamine, alanine, and aspartate. 
These are the ones most easily converted into citric acid cycle intermediates: 
glutamate and glutamine to α-ketoglutarate, 
alanine to pyruvate, and 
aspartate to oxaloacetate.

**Why ammonia is dangerous**
Ammonia is highly toxic, especially to the **brain**.

**Aminotransferases (ALT, AST)**
**ALT (Alanine aminotransferase, GPT)**
**AST (Aspartate aminotransferase, GOT)** **↑ in blood → liver or heart cell injury.** Leak into serum after **heart attack**, **viral/drug-induced hepatitis**, or **toxin exposure** (carbon tetrachloride, chloroform).
**Timing after myocardial infarction (MI):**
AST rises early after MI.
ALT rises later (more liver-specific).
**Creatine Kinase (CK or CPK)**
First enzyme to rise after MI.
Appears quickly and also **disappears quickly**, useful for early diagnosis.
CK-MB isoform is most specific for cardiac injury.
**Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH)**
Elevated in **heart, liver, or muscle damage**.
Rises later than CK and remains high longer.
LDH-1 > LDH-2 (“flip”) suggests **MI**.

<div style="position: absolute; left: 61.65%; top: 1.68%; width: 38.50%; height: 96.83%;">

**Medical Relevance**
**Why these enzymes rise**
Cell injury → loss of membrane integrity → cytosolic enzymes leak into blood.
Measuring specific patterns **helps identify the organ damaged and timing of injury**.
**Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP)**
**↑ in cholestasis**, bile duct obstruction, bone disorders (high osteoblast activity).
**Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (GGT)**
Sensitive marker of **bile duct injury** and **alcohol abuse**.
↑ GGT + ↑ ALP → hepatobiliary disease.
**Amylase & Lipase**
**Amylase**: rises quickly in **acute pancreatitis**.
**Lipase**: more specific for pancreas; stays elevated longer.
