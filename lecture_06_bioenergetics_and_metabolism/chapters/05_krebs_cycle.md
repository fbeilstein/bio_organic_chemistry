**Why a cycle?**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 24.12%; width: 80.35%; height: 57.99%;">

Why is this pathway cyclic and not linear, as we have seen for glycolysis? A simple answer is that it evolved that way, but why would that be advantageous? It turns out that some of the key "intermediates" in the pathway are pulled away for the biosynthesis of other biomolecules. If the citric acid cycle were linear and intermediates pulled off for other reactions, the linear pathway would taper off, which would not be optimal for a key energy production pathway. Of course, removing intermediates from a cyclic pathway would also slow down the process. Still, when this happens, enzymes outside the cycle are used to produce key reaction intermediates that keep the cycle going. The replenishing reactions are called **anaplerotic**.

</div>

</div>

<u>Mechanism</u>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 67.04%; top: 0.83%; width: 32.21%; height: 46.37%;">

Acetyl CoA is a thioester. Hence, it is "high energy" compared to its hydrolysis products. (Remember, there is no such thing as a "high energy" bond.) The free energy released in its hydrolysis is used to drive the reaction forward. This is important. Otherwise, citrate would not be formed readily. This reaction feeds the end product of glycolysis into the citric acid cycle.

<div style="position: absolute; left: 53.00%; top: 39.79%; width: 43.74%; height: 46.68%;">

the chemical rationale for this reaction is to move an OH to a beta position, which in a subsequent step is converted to a β C=O, so it can act as an electron sink to facilitate decarboxylation in a following reaction! <div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](./lecture_06_bioenergetics_and_metabolism/images/slide_82_img_109.png) {left=16.13 top=0.00 width=49.15 height=61.85}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.13%; top: 60.73%; width: 45.14%; height: 39.27%;">

The enzyme has an inorganic Fe4S4 cluster. Each Fe in the cluster coordinates to 4 S2- in a cubane structure, but when either citrate or isocitrate is bound, one of the Fe ions interacts with both the Os of a substrate carboxylate. The other two carboxylates of isocitrate are stabilized through ion-ion interactions by Arg 446 and 663.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 64.16%; top: -0.09%; width: 35.84%; height: 100.00%;">

An active site deprotonated serine abstracts a proton at the S carbon. This is followed by the formation of the C-C double bond and the release of the resulting cis-aconitate from one bond to the FeS cluster.

This cis-aconitate intermediate in the interconversion of citrate and isocitrate undergoes a 180 flip around the C=C double bond (mechanism is still **unknown**). This is followed by rehydration to form the other isomer. The deprotonated His 101 abstracts a hydrogen from a water-bound to the FeS cluster, with the hydroxide acting as a nucleophile, which, along with the redonation of a hydrogen ion on the protonated Ser 642, the alpha-carbon, completes the rehydration step in the formation of the other isomer.

</div>

**5. Succinyl-CoA synthetase (SCS) ****ΔG****o**** = -3.3 kJ/mol**

</div>

![](./lecture_06_bioenergetics_and_metabolism/images/slide_85_img_117.png) {left=16.60 top=63.74 width=49.33 height=28.57}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 66.56%; top: 5.03%; width: 31.73%; height: 36.47%;">

The oxidative decarboxylation of α-ketoglutarate accomplished the same way as another α-ketoacid, pyruvate.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 66.40%; top: 43.21%; width: 33.60%; height: 51.85%;">

This is the first step in which the energy change in the cycle is explicitly captured in the form of a high-energy (with respect to its hydrolysis product) phosphoanhydride bond in the form of GTP (ATP in some organisms). From a chemical step, the cleavage of the thermodynamically unstable thioester is coupled to the endergonic synthesis of GTP. This can transfer its terminal phosphate to ADP to make ATP in a reaction with a ΔGo ≈ 0 kcal/mol.

The succinate dehydrogenase enzyme is part of the larger Complex II of the electron transport chain.

In contrast to NAD+/NADH, the FAD/FADH2 pair stays tightly bound to the enzyme and doesn't readily dissociate. This means that after one cycle of catalysis, the enzyme is functionally "dead". Another oxidizing agent must bind to the enzyme and reoxidize FADH2 back to FAD.

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 51.40%; top: 0.00%; width: 48.60%; height: 95.63%;">

There are Class I (dimers containing an unstable FeS cluster) and Class II (tetramer, no bound iron, oxygen stable) fumarases. Humans possess both cytoplasmic and mitochondrial type II fumarases, which result from the alternative transcription of the fumarase genes. We will consider type II fumarase C from E. Coli.

The tetramer consists solely of α-helices and random coils, featuring two distinct binding sites. Site A appears to be the active site and contains a buried water molecule. Site A, formed from three subunits, binds competitive inhibitors such as citrate and β-(trimethylsilyl)maleate, a cis substrate for fumarase, and is buried. 12 Å away is site B, which is found in only one of the subunits near a pi-helix (H129 through N135) and is more surface-exposed. Each site has a histidine, but mutation of only H188N in the A site disrupts enzyme activity. Both sites bind multi-carboxylates. The role of site B is a bit unclear, but it is most likely an allosteric site involved in the transfer of product (malate) from the buried site to the surface for ultimate dissociation.

</div>
