<div style="position: absolute; left: 18.81%; top: 3.85%; width: 79.08%; height: 93.07%;">

Have their own circular DNA (~16.6 kb in humans).
Encode 13 proteins, 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs (all for oxidative phosphorylation).
Most mitochondrial proteins (>1000) are encoded by nuclear DNA and imported.
Inherited maternally (sperm mitochondria are typically degraded after fertilization).
“Mitochondrial Eve” — the most recent common maternal ancestor, ~150,000–200,000 years ago.
Endosymbiotic origin: descended from an α-proteobacterium engulfed by a proto-eukaryote ~1.5–2 billion years ago.
Constantly undergo fission (splitting) and fusion (joining).
-  Fusion: helps mix contents, complementing damaged mitochondria.
- Fission: helps isolate damaged parts, critical for mitophagy.
Highly dynamic network, not just “static ovals”

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 1.87%; width: 80.35%; height: 95.71%;">

Apoptosis regulation: release of cytochrome c triggers caspase cascade.
Calcium buffering: uptake & release influences signaling and cell death.
Heat generation: brown adipose tissue mitochondria use uncoupling protein (UCP1) to produce heat instead of ATP.
Source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (byproducts of ETC).
Mutations in mitochondrial DNA cause diseases: Leber’s hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), Lethal infantile mitochondrial ophthalmoplegia, Myoclonic epilepsy, Mitochondrial encephalomyopathy lactic acidosis stroke-like episodes (MELAS). Additionally see 3-parents-kid.
Number and shape change with stress, energy demand, aging.
Accumulation of mitochondrial mutations is linked to aging theories (ROS damage hypothesis).

Main site of ATP production (oxidative phosphorylation, ~30–32 ATP per glucose).
Inner membrane forms cristae to increase surface area for electron transport chain (ETC).
Have double membrane structure: Outer membrane permeable (porins). Inner membrane highly selective, contains ETC, impermeable to ions.
Maintain proton gradient (chemiosmosis) driving ATP synthase.

Under aerobic conditions, we continually breathe one of the best oxidizing agents, dioxygen. NADH is oxidized back to NAD+ not directly by dioxygen, but indirectly as electrons flow from NADH through a series of electron carriers to dioxygen, which is then reduced to water. This process is called electron transport. No atoms of oxygen are incorporated into NADH or any intermediary electron carrier. Hence, the enzyme involved in the terminal electron transport step, in which electrons pass to dioxygen, is an oxidase.

Summary of the flow of electrons and protons through the four complexes of the respiratory chain. Electrons reach Q (ubiquinone) through Complexes I and II (as well as through several other paths, e.g. glycerol 3-phosphate dehydrogenase). Reduced Q (QH2) serves as a mobile carrier of electrons and protons. It passes electrons to Complex III, which passes them to another mobile connecting link, cytochrome c. Complex IV then transfers electrons from reduced cytochrome c to O2. Electron flow through Complexes I, III, and IV is accompanied by proton efflux from the matrix into the intermembrane space. In bovine heart, the approximate ratios of Complexes I:II:III:IV are 1.1:1.3:3.0:6.7. Broken lines indicate the diffusion of Q in the plane of the inner membrane, and of cytochrome c through the intermembrane space.

<u>eukaryotic respiratory complex III (3CX5). </u>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 53.16%; top: 3.52%; width: 46.84%; height: 96.48%;">

The subunits involved in electron transfer are cytochrome b, cytochrome c1, and the Rieske iron-sulfur protein (ISP). Cytochrome b has two hemes. One is cyto b562, which is also known as the low-potential heme or cyto bL. The other is cyto b566, the high-potential heme or cyto bH. The cytochrome c1 subunit has one heme.

Proton and electron transfer are coupled processes: the protons in Complex III are carried across the intermembrane space by ubiquinone itself (no channels!). Two reduced ubiquinones (UQH2) from complex I pass their four matrix-derived protons into the intermembrane space. In the process, four electrons are removed in a multiple-step process called the **Q cycle**.

</div>

</div>

The two electrons from each UQH2 take different paths. One electron moves to a Rieske cluster and the other to cytochrome bL. The electrons moved to the Rieske center, then moved to cytochrome c1, and then to the mobile electron carrier cytochrome C, which is bound to the complex in the intermembrane space. The electrons moved to cytochrome bL are transferred to cytochrome bH in the complex. Through this latter path, two electrons (from two UQH2) are moved to oxidized UQ, and two matrix protons are added to reform one UQH2. Hence, only one UQH2 participates in the net reaction

In 1982, several mysterious cases of paralysis came to light in southern California. The victims, some of them teenagers, were frozen like living statues, unable to talk or move. The case was baffling at first, but it was soon traced
to a batch of synthetic heroin that contained MPTP (1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine) as a contaminant. MPTP is rapidly converted in the brain to MPP1 (1-methyl-4-phenylpyridine) by the enzyme monoamine oxidase B. MPP1 is a potent inhibitor of mitochondrial Complex I (NADH–UQ reductase), and it acts preferentially in the substantia nigra, an area of the brain that is essential to movement and also the region of the brain that deteriorates slowly in Parkinson’s disease.
