**Chemical Senses. Taste and Smell.**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 12.66%; width: 38.90%; height: 87.34%;">

**Life Evolved in a Sea of Chemicals**
From the earliest organisms, chemical cues signaled **food, danger, and mating**.
Chemical sensation is the **oldest and most universal** sensory system.
Even bacteria detect chemicals and swim toward nutrients.
**Chemical Detection in Multicellular Life**
Animals must sense chemicals in:
**External environment** (air, water, food)
**Internal environment** (blood, interstitial fluids)
Detection systems diversified over evolution:
Smell → volatile chemicals
Taste → substances in the mouth
Internal chemoreception → hormones, neurotransmitters, blood gases

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 59.33%; top: 17.18%; width: 40.67%; height: 78.30%;">

**Chemoreceptors Throughout the Body**
Specialized cells detect:
Irritants (skin, mucous membranes)
Nutrients (digestive tract)
CO₂ and O₂ levels (carotid bodies)
Acidity (muscles during exertion)
**Gustation & Olfaction**
Work together to form **flavor**.
Strongly connected to **basic drives**: hunger, thirst, emotion, sex, memory.
Despite similar purpose (detect environmental chemicals), they differ in:
Receptor structure
Neural pathways
Behavioral effects
Their neural signals run **in parallel** and merge only at higher cortical levels.

</div>

**Evolutionary Background**
Humans evolved as **omnivores**, requiring a flexible taste system to detect:
**nutrients**
**toxins**
**safe vs. unsafe** foods
Some taste preferences are **innate**:
Attraction to **sweet** (mother’s milk)
Rejection of **bitter** (many plant toxins)
Experience modifies instincts: bitterness (coffee, quinine) can be learned.
The body can detect **nutrient deficiencies** (e.g., salt craving).
**The Five Basic Tastes**
**Sweet** – energy-rich nutrients
**Salty** – essential electrolytes
**Sour** – acids
**Bitter** – potential toxins (detected at extremely low concentrations)
**Umami** – amino acids (glutamate; MSG)

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 54.14%; top: 5.64%; width: 46.18%; height: 82.79%;">

**Chemistry Behind Taste**
Acids → sour
Salts → salty
Many molecules can taste sweet (sugars, proteins like monellin, artificial sweeteners).
Artificial sweeteners can be **10,000–100,000× sweeter** than sucrose.
Bitter substances include ions (K⁺, Mg²⁺) and organic molecules (quinine, caffeine).
**How We Perceive Complex Flavors**
Flavor = **Taste + Smell + Other sensory inputs**
Each food activates a **unique combination** of basic tastes.
Smell greatly shapes flavor (onion tastes similar to apple without smell).
**Texture, temperature, and pain** (e.g., capsaicin) complete the perception

</div>

</div>

**Where Taste Occurs**
Taste is detected not only by the **tongue**, but also by the:
**Palate**
**Pharynx**
**Epiglottis**
Smell contributes through **retronasal olfaction** as food aromas reach the nasal cavity.
**Papillae & Taste Buds**
Papillae = small projections on the tongue:
**Folate** (ridges)
**Vallate** (large “pimples”)
**Fungiform** (mushroom-shaped)
Each papilla contains **1–several hundred taste buds**.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 56.33%; top: 10.54%; width: 43.67%; height: 66.95%;">

**Taste Bud Structure**
Each taste bud contains:
**50–150 taste receptor cells**
**Basal cells**
**Gustatory afferent axons**
Typical person: **2000–5000 taste buds** (range 500–20,000).
**Taste Sensitivity & Thresholds**
Each papilla responds to **one dominant taste** near threshold.
Increasing stimulus concentration → papillae become **less selective**.
Each papilla contains **multiple receptor cell types**, each tuned to a different basic taste.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.58%; top: 1.28%; width: 38.89%; height: 97.44%;">

Evidence suggests additional taste modalities beyond **sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami**.
**1. Fat Taste**
Fat is calorically dense → strong evolutionary value.
Triglycerides stimulate **touch/texture** (oily, creamy), not taste.
**Free fatty acids** activate olfactory & somatosensory systems.
Mice prefer fatty-acid–spiked water.
Specialized **fat-sensitive taste cells** found in mice and humans → likely a **fat taste receptor** exists.
**2. Starch Taste**
Starch = glucose polymer.
Taste is **not** simply glucose detection.
mice without **T1R3** (sweet/umami receptor subunit):
lose sugar preference
**still prefer starch** → likely **dedicated starch receptors**.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 55.30%; top: -0.31%; width: 43.67%; height: 100.62%;">

**3. Carbonation (CO₂) Taste**
CO₂ sensed by:
somatosensory “tingle”
smell
hearing bubbles
Taste cells contain **carbonic anhydrase**, producing **H⁺ → sour**.
But carbonation ≠ sour alone; requires **combined sour + tingling** → a possible **CO₂ taste pathway**.
**4. Calcium Taste**
Animals crave Ca²⁺ when deficient, reject when sated.
Ca²⁺ may taste **bitter + sour**.
Odd finding: Ca²⁺ taste requires **T1R3** in mice; humans’ Ca²⁺ taste is reduced by T1R3 blockers. → possible **calcium-specific taste receptor**.
**5. Water Taste**
Wetness is somatosensory.
Humans report distilled water as “sweet,” “salty,” or “bitter” depending on context.
Insects have clear water receptors.
**No mammalian water-taste receptor identified yet**.

</div>

</div>

**Structure & Location**
Chemically sensitive region = **apical end** with **microvilli** projecting into the **taste pore**
Taste receptor cells are **not neurons** but:
Synapse with **gustatory afferent axons**
Form **electrical & chemical synapses** with **basal cells**
Taste cells constantly renew; **lifespan ≈ 2 weeks**
Degenerate if the sensory nerve is severed

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 54.97%; top: 1.09%; width: 45.18%; height: 89.26%;">

**Signal Transduction**
Chemical stimulus → **receptor potential** (usually depolarizing)
If strong enough → **action potentials**
Depolarization opens **voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels → transmitter release**
**Transmitters by Taste Type**
**Sour & salty:** release **serotonin (5-HT)**
**Sweet, bitter, umami:** release **ATP**
Other transmitters (ACh, GABA, glutamate) present; roles unclear
**Coding & Selectivity**
Most taste receptor cells respond **primarily to one** basic taste
Some taste cells & many gustatory axons are **broadly tuned**
Response patterns depend on **specific transduction mechanisms** expressed in each cell

</div>

**Saltiness**
**Low-Salt Detection (10–150 mM NaCl)**
Salt taste is primarily the taste of **Na⁺**
Low concentrations detected by **amiloride-sensitive Na⁺ channels**
Always open, **voltage-insensitive**
Blocked by **amiloride** (a diuretic)
↑ External Na⁺ → Na⁺ diffuses inward → **depolarization (receptor potential)** → opens voltage-gated Na⁺/Ca²⁺ channels → **transmitter release** onto gustatory afferents
**High-Salt Detection**
Very high salt concentrations taste **bad / aversive**
High salt **activates bitter and sour taste cells** → avoidance
Exact mechanisms still unclear
**Role of Anions**
Anions modify perceived saltiness
**NaCl tastes saltier** than sodium acetate
Larger anions (e.g., acetate) **inhibit Na⁺ salty taste**
Large anions may have their **own taste qualities**
Example: **Sodium saccharin** tastes sweet (anion activates sweet receptors)

**Sourness**
Sour taste = **high acidity / low pH**
Acids (e.g., HCl) release **H⁺ (protons)** in water
**Protons = primary stimulus** for sour taste receptor cells
**How H****+**** triggers sour taste**
**Block K⁺ channels** → reduces K⁺ permeability → **membrane depolarizes** → transmitter release
**Activate or permeate TRP channels** (a sensory ion channel family) → inward **cation current** → depolarization
**Other Possible Mechanisms**
H⁺ may act **both inside and outside** the taste cell
pH shifts can influence **many cellular processes**
Likely that **multiple mechanisms together** create the perception of sourness

**Bitterness**
**Receptor Families**
Bitter, sweet, and umami detection uses **T1R** and **T2R** receptors
All are **G-protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs)**
Functional receptors are **dimers** (two proteins linked together)
**Bitter Receptors**
Humans have **~25 T2R receptor types**
Purpose: **poison detection**
Each bitter taste cell expresses **many or most T2Rs** → Different bitter chemicals = **same “danger” signal**
Brain receives a simple message: **“Bitter = avoid!”**

**Sweetness**
**Sweet Receptors**
Many chemically diverse sweet molecules
**All detected by the *****same***** receptor type**
Sweet receptor = **T1R2 + T1R3** (a GPCR **dimer**)
If either subunit is missing → **no sweetness perception**
Example: **Cats lack T1R2** → cannot taste sweetness
**Why Sweet ≠ Bitter?**
Although they use **the same intracellular pathway**, **receptors are expressed in different taste cells**, which synapse onto **different gustatory axons** → Sweetness and bitterness are sent to the brain along **separate labeled lines**

**Umami (Amino Acids)**
**What Is Umami?**
“Savory” taste of **amino acids**, especially **glutamate**
Many amino acids taste pleasant; some are bitter
Evolutionarily: signals **protein-rich, nutritious foods**
**Umami Receptor**
GPCR **dimer**: **T1R1 + T1R3**
Very similar to sweet receptor (T1R2 + T1R3)
**T1R3 shared** between sweet & umami → **T1R1** determines *amino-acid sensitivity*
Knockout of **T1R1 gene** in mice → **cannot taste glutamate**
**Species Differences**
Many bats lack functional **T1R1** → no umami taste
Vampire bats lack both **sweet and umami receptors**
Loss of these genes is species-specific and evolutionary

**Central taste pathways.**

**• Olfactory receptor neurons use a single main transduction pathway located in their cilia.** **• Sequence of molecular events:**
**Odorant molecules** dissolve in mucus and **bind to odorant receptor proteins** on cilia.
Activated receptors stimulate **Golf (G-protein)**.
Golf activates **adenylyl cyclase**.
Adenylyl cyclase converts ATP → **cAMP**.
**cAMP binds to cyclic-nucleotide–gated (CNG) cation channels**.
**Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ enter** → depolarization begins.
Ca²⁺ opens **Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channels**. • Due to high internal Cl⁻, **Cl⁻ flows OUT**, further **depolarizing** the neuron.
If threshold is reached → **action potentials** fire and travel to the CNS.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 57.99%; top: 18.42%; width: 42.01%; height: 63.16%;">

**Termination & Adaptation**
Odorants **diffuse away** or are **broken down** by mucus enzymes.
Rising **Ca²⁺ activates feedback pathways** that reduce CNG channel activity.
Receptors show **adaptation**: response weakens within ~1 minute even if odor persists.
**Key Features**
**Specialized receptor proteins** initiate signaling.
**cAMP-gated channels** and **Ca²⁺-activated Cl⁻ channels** amplify depolarization.
Olfactory receptor neurons are **true neurons** capable of generating action potentials.

**cAMP-Gated Channels in Olfaction**
Olfactory receptor neurons use an **unusual second-messenger mechanism**.
**cAMP directly opens ion channels** in the olfactory cilia (discovered by *Nakamura & Gold, 1987*).
These **cyclic nucleotide–gated channels**:
Open when **cAMP binds**
Allow **Na⁺ and Ca²⁺ influx → depolarization**
Similar channels are used in **visual transduction**, showing evolutionary reuse of signaling pathways.
**Population Coding of Odors**
Mice have **~1000 odorant receptor types**, each broadly tuned.
**One receptor = responds to many odorants** (with different sensitivity).
**One odorant = activates many receptor types**.
Response strength increases with **odorant concentration**, until saturation.
**Individual receptor cells provide ambiguous signals**.
**The brain interprets the full pattern of activity** across the receptor population to identify odors.

**Olfactory Bulb Organization**
Olfactory receptor neurons send axons to the **paired olfactory bulbs (left and right)**.
Each bulb contains **~2000 glomeruli** (50–200 μm).
Each glomerulus receives:
Input from **~25,000 receptor axons**
Onto **~100 second-order neurons**
Extensive **inhibitory + excitatory circuits** refine odor signals.
**Precise Mapping of Receptor Types**
Axons from receptor cells expressing the **same receptor gene** converge onto **exactly 2 glomeruli** **per bulb**.
Example: neurons expressing receptor **P2** → exactly two P2 glomeruli in each bulb.
Positions are:
**Symmetrical** across left/right bulbs
**Consistent** across individuals
Thus: **glomeruli form an orderly map of expressed receptor genes** → a spatial map of odor identity.

Circuits within/between glomeruli:
Begin **categorizing odors**
Reduce noise from mixture of odorants
Are modulated by **descending cortical inputs**
Final odor identity requires higher-level processing.
**Central Projections**
Olfactory bulb output → **olfactory tract** → multiple brain regions:
**Olfactory cortex** (pyriform cortex)
**Amygdala**
**Entorhinal cortex**
**Unique feature:** olfaction bypasses the thalamus on its primary route to cortex.

Olfactory tubercle → **medial dorsal thalamus** → **orbitofrontal cortex** → mediates **conscious smell perception**.

**Three major principles:**
**Population coding**
**Spatial maps** in the olfactory bulb
**Temporal (spike-timing) codes**
**Population coding:**
No single olfactory receptor can uniquely identify an odor.
Each odorant activates a **unique combination** of receptor types.
The brain interprets the *pattern across the population*.**Example: ** A citrus odor evokes mixed responses in several receptors;  only the *combined pattern* distinguishes it from floral or peppermint.
Receptor cells expressing the same receptor gene project to the **same glomeruli**.
Each odor creates a **reproducible, odor-specific activation pattern** across glomeruli.
**Key idea:** The *position* of active glomeruli forms a **neural map** of the odor.
**Example: ** Minty odor → one pattern; Fruity odor → very different pattern

Unlike vision, hearing, or somatosensation, **odor molecules have no spatial structure**.
So why have maps?
May help organise wiring efficiently
May cluster similar-processing neurons
The map may be a **developmental by-product**, not the code itself
**Still unknown: ** Does the brain *read* these maps to identify odors?
Distinct odors activate **distinct subsets of cortical neurons**.
Example: Octanal → one set of neurons; β-pinene + hexanal → mostly different sets
This shows that **odor identity persists** beyond the bulb as a pattern across many neurons.

**Chemical senses as a foundation**
Taste and smell give access to the *most fundamental* sensory computations.
Despite detecting thousands of chemicals, they rely on **universal cellular mechanisms** — the same second-messenger systems used in neurotransmission, development, and fertilization.
**Shared principles across sensory systems**
**Broad tuning**: individual sensory cells respond to many stimuli.
**Population coding**: the brain extracts meaning from patterns across *many* neurons.
**Spatial maps**: sensory information is organized into orderly maps—though in olfaction the map reflects **chemical identity**, not physical space.
**Temporal coding**: the timing of spikes enriches sensory representations and may enable fine discrimination.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 54.97%; top: 8.80%; width: 45.18%; height: 70.73%;">

**Unity beneath specialization**
Even highly specialized senses—vision, hearing, touch—reuse **similar molecular logic**.
Smell and vision, for example, both rely on **cyclic nucleotide–gated channels** and **G-protein–coupled receptors**.
Across systems, the brain uses the same strategies to create rich, precise perception from noisy receptors.
**Looking ahead**
These coding principles—population patterns, spatial organization, and spike timing—reappear throughout the sensory chapters.
As we move to light, sound, and mechanical sensation, you’ll see the **same computational themes** expressed in remarkably different biological hardware.

</div>

**3. Olfactory Variation: “Cut Grass” Smell (cis-3-hexen-1-ol)**
**Gene:** **OR2J3**
**Phenotype:**
Some people perceive strong fresh-cut grass / green leaf odor
A pair of two single-nucleotide polymorphisms strongly reduce sensitivity to this odorant.
**4. Grapefruit / Citrus Differences (Limonene Enantiomers)**
**Genes:** OR2J3, OR1A1, others
**Chemistry:**
**(+)-limonene** → orange/citrus (oranges)
**(–)-limonene** → pine/turpentine (bergamot)
**Phenotype:**
People differ in sensitivity and perceived pleasantness
**5. Androstenone (“Sweaty vs Floral”)**
**Gene:** **OR7D4**
**Phenotype:**
Some smell “sweaty/urinous”
Others smell “sweet/vanilla-like”
Some anosmic
**The first mammalian pheromone to be identified (when inhaled by a female pig that is in heat, results in the female assuming the mating stance)**
**active ingredient in 'Boarmate', a commercial product made by DuPont sold to pig farmers**

My dog is craving sweets. The problem is that dogs metabolize methylxanthines — especially theobromine and caffeine — poorly. From your biochemistry course, you know that theobromine is abundant in cocoa powder (*Theobroma cacao*) and caffeine is found in coffee, thus present in many sweets. So we should be concerned whether the dog has not ingested too much. 

To estimate a safe limit, you look at veterinary toxicology data (<u>https://www.aspcapro.org/sites/default/files/m-toxbrief_0201.pdf</u>): mild signs of toxicity begin around 20 mg/kg. To stay well below that, you set a conservative safety threshold of 5 mg/kg (25%). Your dog weighs 30 kg. Your dog’s favorite sweets list no more than 3 g/kg of total methylxanthines (the actual amount is expected to be lower). Each portion weighs 15 g. What is the maximum number of portions your dog could safely eat without exceeding your chosen threshold?
