**soma **(3-18 micrometers) is the body of the neuron that  contains the nucleus and is responsible for  most protein synthesis.

**dendrites **of a neuron are cellular extensions with many branches. Dendrites receive electrical and chemical signals from other neurons through synapses and conduct impulses toward the cell body.

**axons **are a fine projections that can extend tens, or even tens of thousands of times the diameter of the soma in length. The axon primarily carries nerve signals away from the soma. Many neurons have only one axon, but this axon may—and usually will—undergo extensive branching, enabling communication with many target cells. The part of the axon where it emerges from the soma is called the **axon hillock. **

The **axon terminal** is a structure at the end of the axon farthest from the soma and contains **synapses**. Synaptic boutons are specialized structures where neurotransmitter chemicals are released to communicate with target neurons.

Most neurons can be anatomically characterized as:

**Unipolar**: single process. Unipolar cells are exclusively sensory neurons (the nerve cells that are activated by sensory input from the environment). 
**Bipolar**: 1 axon and 1 dendrite (retina- vision, olfactory epithelium - sense of smell, inner ear - cochlea and vestibular system) 
**Multipolar**: 1 axon and 2 or more dendrites (brain and spinal cord)
**Anaxonic**: where the axon cannot be distinguished from the dendrite(s) (retina and olfactory bulb - smell)
**Pseudounipolar**: 1 process which then serves as both an axon and a dendrite

Like all animal cells, the cell body of every neuron is enclosed by a plasma membrane, a **bilayer **of lipid molecules with many types of embedded protein structures. A lipid bilayer is a powerful **electrical insulator**, but in neurons, many of the protein structures embedded in the membrane are electrically active. 

These include ion **channels **that permit electrically charged ions to flow across the membrane and ion pumps that chemically transport ions from one side of the membrane to the other. Most ion channels are gated, permeable only to specific types of ions. Some ion channels are **voltage gated**, meaning that they can be switched between open and closed states by altering the voltage difference across the membrane. Others are chemically gated, meaning that they can be switched between open and closed states by interactions with chemicals that diffuse through the extracellular fluid. The ions include Na2+, K+, Cl-, and Ca2+.

Another important structures embedded in the membrane are **sodium–potassium pumps**.

Sodium–potassium pump

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.95%; top: 8.05%; width: 65.51%; height: 6.88%;">

discovered in 1957 by the Danish scientist Jens Christian Skou (Nobel Prize in 1997)

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 9.84%; top: 14.44%; width: 85.47%; height: 35.91%;">

The pump uses the energy released from the hydrolysis of one molecule of ATP. It transports three sodium ions 3 Na+ out of the cell and two potassium ions 2 K+ into the cell for each ATP molecule consumed. Thus, there is a net export of a single positive charge per pump cycle. The net effect is an extracellular concentration of sodium ions which is **5 times** the intracellular concentration, and an intracellular concentration of potassium ions which is **30 times** the extracellular concentration.

**Thus the Na+/K+-ATPase helps maintain resting potential, affects transport, and regulates cellular volume.**

Detailed molecular studies have revealed that all voltage-gated cation channels—those permeant to *K**+*,
*Na**+*, or *Ca**2*+—have a similar underlying architecture. In fact, there is now strong evidence from studies of bacteria, plants, invertebrates, and vertebrates that most voltage-sensitive cation channels stem from a **common**
**ancestral channel**—perhaps a K+ channel—that can be traced to a single-cell organism living more than 1.4
billion years ago, before the evolution of separate plant and animal kingdoms.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.19%; top: 4.60%; width: 53.53%; height: 7.48%;">

Voltage-gated channels

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.19%; top: 34.15%; width: 53.15%; height: 29.93%;">

Voltage-gated cation channels are composed of pore-forming α-subunits that each contain a motif consisting of six transmembrane segments (S1–S6). A seventh hydrophobic region, the **P-region (pore loop)**, connects the S5 and S6 segments. It forms a loop that dips into and out of the extracellular side of the membrane and forms the channel’s **selectivity filter**. *K**+* and *Na**+* have different radii 1.33 Å and 0.95 Å respectively, moreover they are hydrated. As a result **ion size **and charge density could be used to make passage of one of them more energetically unfavorable.

</div>

![](images/slide_7_img_11.png) {left=68.63 top=32.26 width=30.79 height=65.19}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.38%; top: 68.08%; width: 53.15%; height: 29.93%;">

**The S4 segment** is thought to play a particularly important role in voltage-gating. This transmembrane segment contains a distinctive pattern of amino acids in which every third position contains a positively charged arginine or lysine residue. Because this pattern of positive charges is present in all voltage-gated channels but is absent in channels that are not voltage-gated, it is thought that this region is the voltage sensor — that part of the protein that transduces depolarization of the cell membrane into a conformational change that opens the channel.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.61%; top: 8.78%; width: 49.20%; height: 16.76%;">

A large single protein with **four domains**, each containing **six transmembrane segments (S1–S6)**. The **S4 segment** acts as a **voltage sensor** — it has positively charged residues that move when the membrane depolarizes.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.38%; top: 29.27%; width: 51.28%; height: 23.34%;">

During depolarization it has acts like this:
**Activation stage** — opens rapidly when the cell depolarizes. Depolarization twists S4, and this conformational change in the molecule causes the gate to open.
**Inactivation stage** — closes shortly after opening, even if the membrane is still depolarized.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.88%; top: 58.89%; width: 75.73%; height: 39.50%;">

Function:
**Resting state**: Activation gate closed, inactivation gate open (no Na⁺ flow).
**Depolarization**: Voltage change opens activation gate almost instantly: Na⁺ rushes into the cell (down its gradient).This further depolarizes the membrane (positive feedback).
**Inactivation**: Within ~1 ms, when inactivation gate closes, channel is inactivated (can’t reopen until repolarized).
**Repolarization**: When the membrane returns to negative potential, inactivation gate opens again and activation gate closes (reset).

Fast inward Na⁺ current causes sharp spike in membrane potential.

</div>

Neuron membrane is a capacitor

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.99%; top: 6.88%; width: 78.92%; height: 23.34%;">

The lipid bilayer is largely impermeable to ions.
The intracellular and extracellular concentrations of K+, Na+ and Cl− differ significantly.
Ion channels (both voltage-gated and non–voltage-gated) are selective for specific ions.
An electric potential difference exists across the membrane.
Diffusive and electrical forces together maintain a dynamic equilibrium of ionic fluxes.

![](images/slide_20_img_40.png) {left=60.29 top=55.59 width=36.62 height=43.89}

**Electrochemical equilibrium.**
If the inside and outside concentrations are different, diffusion tries to make them equal. You need an electric force (equilibrium potential) to oppose diffusion in order maintain concentrations.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.70%; top: 39.45%; width: 80.18%; height: 58.48%;">

According to the Hodgkin-Huxley model an action potential involves the following sequence of events. Depolarization of the membrane causes *Na**+* channels to open rapidly (an increase in gNa), resulting in an inward *Na**+* current. This current, by discharging the membrane capacitance, causes further depolarization, thereby opening more *Na**+* channels, resulting in a further increase in inward current. This regenerative process drives the membrane potential toward *E**Na* , causing the rising phase of the action potential. 

The depolarization limits the duration of the action potential in two ways: 
It gradually inactivates the voltage-gated *Na**+ *channels, thus reducing gNa
It opens, with some delay, the voltage-gated *K**+* channels, thereby increasing gK. Consequently, the inward *Na*+ current is followed by an outward *K**+* current that tends to repolarize the membrane.

The combined effect of this transient increase in *K**+** *conductance and the residual inactivation of the* Na**+* channels underlies the **absolute refractory period**, the brief period of time following an action potential when it is impossible to elicit another action potential. As some *K**+* channels begin to close and some *Na**+* channels recover from inactivation, the membrane enters the **relative refractory period**, during which it is possible to trigger an action potential, but only by applying stimuli that are stronger than those normally required to reach threshold.

Co-release and co-transmission are distinct modes of release: 
(a) Co-release, both neurotransmitters (mixed red and blue) are packaged into the same set of synaptic vesicles.

Normally, after dopamine is released into the synaptic cleft, DAT pumps it back into the presynaptic neuron.

Dopamine accumulates in the synaptic cleft.
Persistent stimulation of **D1, D2-family dopamine receptors** on postsynaptic neurons occurs.
**Functional effect**
Excess dopamine leads to strong activation of neural reward pathways 
Produces euphoria, heightened energy, increased locomotor activity
Also increases sympathetic activity (via norepinephrine effects): tachycardia, hypertension, mydriasis

Much of the evidence leading to the present understanding of chemical synaptic transmission was obtained from experiments examining the release of ACh at **neuromuscular junctions**. Such synapses occur at specializations called **end plates**.

**Rapid freezing and electron microscopy **were developed in the 1960-70s by J.Heuser and T.Reese.

Stimulate the neuron electrically to trigger vesicle release.

**Rapid freezing**
The biological sample (e.g., neurons or synapses) is frozen extremely fast — typically with liquid nitrogen or liquid helium — to prevent formation of large ice crystals that would damage the structure.
**Fracture**
The frozen specimen is cracked open inside a vacuum chamber. The fracture plane tends to follow weak points, especially between the lipid bilayers of cell membranes. This reveals the inner faces of the membrane (cytoplasmic and extracellular leaflets).
**Etching (optional)**
The sample is slightly warmed (sublimated) to remove a thin layer of surface ice — this enhances relief and contrast of structures like vesicles, channels, or proteins embedded in the membrane.
**Metal shadowing**
The exposed surface is coated with a thin layer of platinum or gold, followed by a carbon layer for stability. This creates a replica of the fractured surface.
**Replica imaging**
The biological material is dissolved away, leaving only the metal replica, which is then observed under an electron microscope.

**Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging** (fMRI) using the Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (**BOLD**) contrast is a noninvasive technique that maps brain activity by detecting changes in blood oxygenation. When a brain region becomes more active, it consumes more oxygen, causing an initial, small drop in oxygenated hemoglobin followed by a surge of blood flow that delivers a surplus of oxygenated hemoglobin. Because oxygenated hemoglobin has different magnetic properties than deoxygenated hemoglobin (**paramagnetic vs diamagnetic**), this difference is measured as a signal change, creating a map of neural activity.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.14%; top: 57.04%; width: 82.97%; height: 39.80%;">

**Neural activity**: When a person performs a task, neurons in a specific brain region become active.
**Initial response**: This activity initially increases the demand for oxygen, leading to a slight decrease in oxygenated hemoglobin and a rise in deoxygenated hemoglobin.
**Blood flow increase**: A few seconds later, the brain compensates by increasing blood flow to that region, which delivers more oxygenated hemoglobin than is consumed.
**BOLD signal**: This large increase in oxygenated hemoglobin compared to deoxygenated hemoglobin is what the BOLD technique detects.
**Paramagnetic properties**: Deoxygenated hemoglobin is paramagnetic, meaning it affects the local magnetic field, while oxygenated hemoglobin is not. This difference in magnetic properties allows the fMRI machine to detect the changes in the BOLD signal.

</div>

**Electroencephalography** (EEG) is a non-invasive method to record an electrogram of the electrical activity of the brain. The bio signals detected by EEG have been shown to represent the postsynaptic potentials of pyramidal neurons in the neocortex and allocortex. Limitations: noisy signals and poor resolution for deeper layers.

<div style="position: absolute; left: 13.21%; top: 6.61%; width: 59.81%; height: 21.55%;">

**Magnetoencephalography **(MEG) is a non-invasive medical imaging technique that measures the magnetic fields produced by the brain's electrical activity using a sensitive device called a superconducting quantum interference device (SQUID). It is used to identify the source of brain activity with high temporal resolution (on the order of milliseconds).
