Electrical synapses are less common than chemical synapses
Extremely fast (fractions of ms)
Allow bidirectional transmission (some connexons have special features for unidirectional transmission)
The brainstem neurons that generate rhythmic electrical activity underlying breathing are synchronized by electrical synapses, as are populations of interneurons in the cerebral cortex, thalamus, cerebellum, and other brain regions.
As connexon pores are large enough to allow second messengers to diffuse between cells also permits electrical synapses to synchronize the intracellular signaling of coupled cells. This feature may be particularly important for glial cells, which form large intracellular signaling networks via their gap junctions.

Dual-transmitter neurons

</div>

![](./lecture_10_electrophysiology/images/slide_62_img_217.png) {left=21.12 top=12.88 width=72.01 height=48.31}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 18.56%; top: 65.71%; width: 79.88%; height: 26.63%;">

Co-release and co-transmission are distinct modes of release: 
(a) Co-release, both neurotransmitters (mixed red and blue) are packaged into the same set of synaptic vesicles. 
(b) Co-transmission: neurotransmitters are sequestered into distinct populations of synaptic vesicles with differential release mediated by differential Ca2+ sensitivities (left panel). For example, a single action potential might release one set of vesicles (red), but multiple action potentials might be required to release both sets of vesicles (red and blue). Alternatively, co-transmission can rely on spatial segregation of vesicle populations to different boutons (right panel) in which case, unique information is transmitted to different postsynaptic targets.

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 13.93%; top: 0.00%; width: 84.36%; height: 33.22%;">

As of current knowledge, there are over **100 **known neurotransmitters in the nervous system. They can be broadly classified into **small-molecule neurotransmitters **(*GABA, ACh, Glutamate*) and **neuropeptides **(*Substance P, Endorphins, Oxytocin*). In some cases, neurons synthesize and release two or more different neurotransmitters (**co-transmitters)**.

</div>

![](./lecture_10_electrophysiology/images/slide_63_img_218.png) {left=13.15 top=19.64 width=39.14 height=47.33}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 13.93%; top: 72.35%; width: 82.54%; height: 26.63%;">

After a neurotransmitter has been secreted into the synaptic cleft via 
diffusion away from the postsynaptic receptors, 
reuptake into nerve terminals or surrounding glial cells, 
degradation by specific enzymes,
combination of these mechanisms.
 Specific transporter proteins remove most small-molecule neurotransmitters (or their metabolites) from the synaptic cleft, ultimately delivering them back to the presynaptic terminal for reuse.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 50.46%; top: 14.25%; width: 49.54%; height: 39.80%;">

Small-molecule neurotransmitters :
Local production in synapses
Precursor molecules required to make new molecules of neurotransmitter are usually Taken into the nerve terminal by transporters found in the plasma membrane
Enzymes that synthesize these neurotransmitters are present in the cytoplasm of the presynaptic terminal
Newly synthesized transmitters are then loaded into **small clear-core vesicles** (40-60 nm) via another type of transporter located in the vesicular membrane.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 51.57%; top: 50.00%; width: 47.93%; height: 23.34%;">

Neuropeptides:
synthesized in the cell body of a neuron
packaged into synaptic vesicles (90-250 nm in diameter) -  **large dense-core vesicles **(appear electron-dense in EM**)**.
Peptide-filled vesicles are transported along an axon and down to the synaptic terminal via axonal transport.

</div>

</div>

**Amino acids:**
Glutamate (Glu) - main excitatory neurotransmitter in the CNS, learning, memory, cognition.
GABA (Gamma-aminobutyric acid) - main inhibitory neurotransmitter, suppresses neuronal firing, reduces anxiety

**Biogenic Amines:**
Dopamine - reward, motivation, reinforcement learning, motor control (deficiency → Parkinson’s), excess signaling → psychosis (dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia)
Serotonin (5-HT) - mood, anxiety, satiety, sleep, pain processing, serotonin boost → antidepressants
Norepinephrine (Noradrenaline) - arousal, attention, focus, vigilance, stress response
Epinephrine (Adrenaline) - stress, cardiovascular response
Acetylcholine - attention, memory, learning, loss in Alzheimer’s disease

**Purinergic :**
ATP - stimulates glial cells
ADP - modulates vascular tone and platelet function
Adenosine - sleep, suppresses arousal, protects brain during hypoxia

**Peptide:**
Endorphins / Enkephalins / Dynorphins - pain relief, reward, stress modulation
Substance P - pain transmission, neurogenic inflammation
Oxytocin / Vasopressin - social bonding, trust, stress, maternal behavior

**Other:**
Endocannabinoids - regulate appetite, mood, pain, reward
Nitric Oxide (NO) - diffuses across membranes, vasodilation, synaptic plasticity

Among the many interesting drugs that interact with cholinergic enzymes are the **organophosphates**. One such compound is the nerve gas **sarin** (1995 Tokyo terrorist attack). Organophosphates can be lethal because they inhibit AChE, allowing ACh to accumulate at cholinergic synapses. This buildup of ACh depolarizes the postsynaptic muscle cell and renders it refractory to subsequent ACh release, causing neuromuscular paralysis and other effects. The high sensitivity of insects to AChE inhibitors has made organophosphates popular **insecticides**.

</div>

Sarin (red), acetylcholinesterase (yellow), acetylcholine (blue)

![](./lecture_10_electrophysiology/images/slide_68_img_234.png) {left=19.12 top=66.26 width=37.39 height=26.62}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.99%; top: 2.16%; width: 82.98%; height: 26.63%;">

Much of the evidence leading to the present understanding of chemical synaptic transmission was obtained from experiments examining the release of ACh at **neuromuscular junctions**. Such synapses occur at specializations called **end plates**. 
Most of the pioneering work was performed during the 1950-60s by B. Katz and his collaborators by exploring frog neuromuscular junction. When an intracellular microelectrode is used to record the membrane potential of a muscle cell, an action potential in the presynaptic motor neuron can be seen to elicit depolarization of the postsynaptic muscle fiber (**end plate potential (EPP)**).

</div>

![](./lecture_10_electrophysiology/images/slide_75_img_265.png) {left=45.90 top=32.80 width=19.92 height=28.50}

![](./lecture_10_electrophysiology/images/slide_75_img_266.png) {left=19.96 top=32.08 width=22.52 height=26.93}

![](./lecture_10_electrophysiology/images/slide_75_img_267.png) {left=69.25 top=30.58 width=15.88 height=29.93}

![](./lecture_10_electrophysiology/images/slide_75_img_268.png) {left=18.91 top=62.01 width=26.51 height=36.28}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 49.08%; top: 67.96%; width: 49.25%; height: 23.34%;">

Katz found that spontaneous changes in muscle cell membrane potential occur even in the absence of stimulation of the presynaptic motor neuron. These changes have the same shape as EPPs but are much smaller (1 mV instead of more than 50 mV). Those fluctuations were called **miniature end plate potentials**, or **MEPPs**.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 56.30%; top: 3.16%; width: 42.10%; height: 16.76%;">

The experiments have shown that increments in the EPP response occur in units about the size of single MEPPs. These “**quantal**” fluctuations in the amplitude of EPPs are made up of individual units, each equivalent to a MEPP.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 56.30%; top: 21.23%; width: 43.70%; height: 33.22%;">

The idea that EPPs represent the simultaneous release of many MEPP-like units was tested statistically. A method of statistical analysis based on the independent occurrence of unitary events (**Poisson statistics**) predicts what the distribution of EPP amplitudes would look like during a large number of trials of motor neuron stimulation, under the assumption that EPPs are built up from unitary events represented by MEPPs.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 57.38%; top: 55.06%; width: 38.16%; height: 16.76%;">

An emerging experimental technique of **electron microscop**y revealed that the synaptic vesicles loaded with transmitter are the source of the quanta.

**Transmission electron microscopy** (TEM) is a microscopy technique in which a beam of electrons is transmitted through a specimen to form an image. The specimen is most often an ultrathin section less than **100 nm thick**.

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.09%; top: 7.44%; width: 55.20%; height: 51.22%;">

**Rapid freezing and electron microscopy **were developed in the 1960-70s by J.Heuser and T.Reese.
The method:
Stimulate the neuron electrically to trigger vesicle release.
After a controlled delay (on the order of milliseconds), the tissue is “slam-frozen” against a cooled metal block at liquid helium or liquid nitrogen temperatures (~–180 °C).
This instantly halts all biological motion, “locking” vesicles mid-fusion.
The frozen tissue is then fractured, fixed, and imaged with **electron microscopy (EM)**.

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.50%; top: 13.63%; width: 42.54%; height: 82.60%;">

**Rapid freezing**
The biological sample (e.g., neurons or synapses) is frozen extremely fast — typically with liquid nitrogen or liquid helium — to prevent formation of large ice crystals that would damage the structure.
**Fracture**
The frozen specimen is cracked open inside a vacuum chamber. The fracture plane tends to follow weak points, especially between the lipid bilayers of cell membranes. This reveals the inner faces of the membrane (cytoplasmic and extracellular leaflets).
**Etching (optional)**
The sample is slightly warmed (sublimated) to remove a thin layer of surface ice — this enhances relief and contrast of structures like vesicles, channels, or proteins embedded in the membrane.
**Metal shadowing**
The exposed surface is coated with a thin layer of platinum or gold, followed by a carbon layer for stability. This creates a replica of the fractured surface.
**Replica imaging**
The biological material is dissolved away, leaving only the metal replica, which is then observed under an electron microscope.

Exocytosis can dramatically increase the surface area of presynaptic terminals, this extra membrane is removed within a **few minutes**. The experiments, again carried out at the frog neuromuscular junction, were based on filling the synaptic cleft with horseradish peroxidase (HRP), an enzyme that produces a dense reaction product that is visible in an electron microscope. The precursors to synaptic vesicles originally are produced in the **endoplasmic reticulum** and** Golgi apparatus **in the neuronal cell body. Long distance between the cell body and the presynaptic terminal in most neurons, transport of vesicles from the soma would not permit rapid replenishment.  Thus, local recycling is well suited to the peculiar anatomy of neurons.

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.24%; top: 3.95%; width: 66.06%; height: 39.80%;">

The protein **synapsin**, reversibly binds to synaptic vesicles, keeping these vesicles tethered within the reserve pool by cross-linking vesicles to each other. Mobilization of these reserve pool vesicles is caused by **phosphorylation** of synapsin by proteins kinases, most notably the **Ca****2+****/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase, type II** (CaMKII), which allows synapsin to dissociate from the vesicles. 

Vesicles make their way to the plasma membrane and are then attached to this membrane by docking reactions that involve **SNARE **proteins. A series of priming reactions then prepares the vesicular and plasma membranes for fusio that involve such proteins as **SNAPs, ATPase NSF, munc13, munc18, complexin, snapin, syntaphilin, and tomosyn.**

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.75%; top: 51.49%; width: 58.92%; height: 49.68%;">

Molecular mechanisms of exocytosis during neurotransmitter release.
**(a)** **Structure of the SNARE complex.** 
The **vesicular SNARE**, **synaptobrevin **(blue), forms a helical complex with the plasma **membrane SNAREs syntaxin** (red) and **SNAP-25** (green). Also shown is the structure of **synaptotagmin**, a vesicular Ca2+-binding protein, with bound Ca2+ indicated by spheres. 

**(b)** **A model for Ca****2+****-triggered vesicle fusion.** 
During docking of the synaptic vesicle, SNARE proteins on the vesicle and plasma membranes form a complex (as in A) that brings together the two membranes. Synaptotagmin binds to this SNARE complex. Subsequent binding of Ca2+  to synaptotagmin causes the cytoplasmic region of this protein to insert into the plasma membrane to produce the membrane curvature that catalyzes membrane fusion.

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 13.16%; top: 2.13%; width: 84.74%; height: 20.05%;">

**Synaptotagmin** to act as a Ca2+ sensor that triggers vesicle fusion by signaling the elevation of Ca2+ within the terminal. Thus, SNARE proteins bring the two membranes close together, while Ca2+-induced changes in synaptotagmin then produce the final curvature that enables **exocytosis**.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 13.83%; top: 18.33%; width: 85.70%; height: 23.34%;">

An important protein involved in **endocytotic **budding of vesicles from the plasma membrane is **clathrin**. Clathrin has a unique structure that is called a **triskelion **because of its three-legged appearance. Several adaptor proteins, such as **AP-2 and AP-180**, connect clathrin to the proteins and lipids of this membrane. These adaptor proteins, as well as other proteins such as **amphiphysin, epsin, and Eps-15,** help assemble individual triskelia into structures that resemble geodesic domes.

</div>

![](./lecture_10_electrophysiology/images/slide_85_img_293.png) {left=61.23 top=43.90 width=38.30 height=48.44}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.82%; top: 50.00%; width: 42.44%; height: 46.39%;">

**Dynamin**, forms a ringlike coil that surrounds the lipid stalk. This coil causes the final pinching-off of membrane. Coated vesicles then are transported away from the plasma membrane by the cytoskeletal protein **actin**. This allows the clathrin coats to be removed by an **ATPase, Hsc70**, **auxilin**, **synaptojanin**. Uncoated vesicles can then continue their journey through the recycling process, eventually becoming refilled with neurotransmitter due to the actions of neurotransmitter transporters in the vesicle membrane. These transporters exchange protons within the vesicle for neurotransmitter; the acidic interior of the vesicle is produced by a proton pump that also is located in the vesicle membrane.
