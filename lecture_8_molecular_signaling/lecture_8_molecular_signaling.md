**No cell lives in isolation**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 15.40%; width: 80.35%; height: 80.53%;">

Many cells sense physical stimuli: mechanical pressure (touch), heat, light, variety of environmental chemicals, including nutrients such as sugars and amino
acids as well as oxygen, toxic compounds, and diverse molecules that convey taste (tastants) and odor (odorants), levels of oxygen, etc.

Many types of cells release particular chemicals that can influence the behavior of another cell; these are often called **extracellular** signaling molecules, or simply **signals**. You can think about signals in the brain or hormones.

In the vertebrate eye, light is transmitted through the transparent corneal tissue and focused by the lens tissue, eventually impinging on the neural retina. The precise arrangement of tissues in the eye cannot be disturbed without impairing its function. Such coordination in the construction of the lens and retina is accomplished by one group of cells communicating an organizing change in the behavior or developmental trajectory of an adjacent set of cells. This kind of interaction is known as an **induction**.

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 10.60%;">

**No cell lives in isolation**

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 14.41%; width: 80.35%; height: 81.85%;">

The cell that receives a signal — termed the **target cell** — must be able to
detect the signaling molecule. Typically, the signaling molecule binds
noncovalently to a specific protein in the cell, typically called the
**receptor**, that contains a binding site for the signaling molecule. The
signaling molecule that binds a receptor is often called the **ligand **of the
receptor. Receptors bind a single type of molecule or a group of closely
related molecules.

The release and reception of such signals is a fundamental process, known as **cellular communication**, that shapes the development and function of every living organism.

</div>

</div>

**Generalized signal transduction pathway.** The series of steps leading from initial detection to the final **response **is termed a **signal transduction pathway**. Signal transduction pathways ultimately lead to activation (or in some cases inhibition) of one or more **effector proteins** — often enzymes or transcription factors or cytoskeletal proteins — that lead directly to changes in cellular activities

</div>

**Essential features of most signal transduction pathways**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 20.32%; width: 80.35%; height: 74.62%;">

**Signal amplification **
the activation of a single receptor molecule can lead to activation of more than one molecule of a downstream intermediate protein in the signal transduction pathway. Each intermediate protein can also activate multiple proteins downstream of it, resulting in a cascade of activation. The extent of amplification depends on the number of steps in the signal transduction cascade and on the relative concentrations of the various components.
**Feedback repression**
Cells must be able to suppress or even turn off a signal transduction
pathway once the cell has adequately responded to the signal or the signal
has been removed. Virtually all signal transduction pathways have
feedback controls that will shut down the pathway: once activated, an
effector protein modifies either the receptor or one of the proteins that
operates in the early stages of the signal transduction pathway, thus
blocking the downstream cellular responses

</div>

In **endocrine **signaling, the signaling molecules (**hormones**) act on target cells distant from their site of synthesis. Examples: insulin, epinephrine, etc.

In **paracrine **signaling, the signaling molecules released by a cell affect only those target cells in close proximity (few micrometers). Examples: a neuron releasing a neurotransmitter that acts on an adjacent neuron, many of the protein growth factors that regulate development in multicellular organisms act at short range. In some cases, paracrine signaling molecules, e.g. TGF-β, are secreted by a cell and then trapped nearby in the extracellular matrix until freed to bind to cell-surface receptors on a nearby cell.

In **autocrine **signaling, cells respond to substances that they themselves release. This type of signaling is particularly characteristic of tumor cells. Many tumor cells release growth factors that stimulate inappropriate, unregulated self-proliferation.

Some signaling molecules can act at both short and long ranges. For example, epinephrine (also known as adrenaline) functions as a neurotransmitter (paracrine signaling) and as a hormone (endocrine signaling).

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.52%; width: 80.35%; height: 89.77%;">

The effector proteins in signal transduction pathways are often enzymes or
transcription factors that induce two major types of cellular responses: 
rapid **short-term** changes in protein activities (seconds-to-minutes) or 
slower **long-term** changes (hours-to-days). 

**Short-term ****changes** are typically consequences of modifications to specific preexisting enzymes and other proteins that alter their activity or function, often by covalent modifications such as phosphorylation or ubiquitination or by binding of ions or molecules such as or cAMP. Such modifications can induce changes in cellular metabolism of sugars, amino acids, and lipids; secretion of hormones; electric signals called action potentials. 

**Long-term changes** are typically consequences of changes in gene expression, either activating or inhibiting synthesis of specific proteins. The effector proteins are usually transcription factors. Long-term changes in the amounts and activities of specific proteins within cells are critical for cell proliferation, cell differentiation, and organismal development.

</div>

</div>

Cellular signaling controls various aspects of multicellular life forms. Not only the key biological processes such as cell division, differentiation, growth, and cell-cycle transition, but also specialized cell-specific functions such as neurotransmission, pathogen-sensing, phagocytosis, and antigen-presentation are controlled by specific signaling pathways. The process of autophagy and nutrient cycling and recycling are some accessory pathways that are triggered by definitive signaling cues.

The interacting surfaces of a receptor and ligand are **complementary** (see image) held together by multiple weak, noncovalent forces, including ionic, van der Waals, and hydrophobic interactions. 

Most cases, the binding of the ligand to the receptor causes the receptor to change from an inactive conformation (R) to an active conformation R*.
R + L → RL → LR*
Only the active conformation of the receptor R* can trigger the subsequent steps in the signal transduction pathway. Thus receptors are considered to be **allosteric proteins.**
 
As with all allosteric proteins, there is an equilibrium between the R and R* forms of the receptor. The ligand binds much more tightly to the R* than the R conformation, thus pushing the equilibrium to the right and activating the signal transduction pathway.

The **minimum effective concentration** (MEC) is the lower boundary for effective drug concentrations; plasma concentrations below the MEC have a high probability of being subtherapeutic. 
The **maximum tolerated concentration** (MTC) is the upper boundary for optimum drug concentrations; plasma concentrations above the MTC have a high probability of producing adverse effects or toxicity.
The **onset of action** of a drug, which may be estimated as the time it takes for plasma concentrations to reach the MEC.
The **duration of action** of a drug, which may be estimated as the time during which plasma concentrations remain within the therapeutic range.

A **full agonist** is a ligand or drug that is capable of binding to and activating a receptor with maximal efficacy (Epinephrine + adrenergic receptor). 
A **partial agonist** also can activate a receptor upon binding,but it elicits a reduced receptor response compared to a full agonist, even with increasing concentration of the ligand (Buprenorphine + mu-opioid receptor, good because decreases the risk of overdose).
An **inverse agonist** is a drug that has the same binding site as a full agonist but reduces activity in a receptor that would otherwise be **constitutively active** (activation without ligand). Example: Antihistamines + histamine receptors.
An **antagonist** is a ligand that blocks the activation of the receptor by preventing agonists from binding. In contrast to the inverse agonist, an antagonist does not have any intrinsic activity on the receptor itself, it simply blocks agonists from interacting with the receptor (caffeine is an antagonist at adenosine receptors).
**Allosteric modulators**, can regulate receptor activity without interacting with a receptor’s orthosteric site,

Control the flow of ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) across membranes → change membrane potential, trigger electrical or chemical responses.
Mediate synaptic transmission and neuronal excitability.
Targeted by many drugs (benzodiazepines, anesthetics, anticonvulsants).

**Types of Ion Channels**

**1. Ligand-Gated (Ionotropic)**
Opened directly by neurotransmitter binding (e.g., acetylcholine, GABA, glutamate).
Fast synaptic signaling.
Examples: nAChR, GABA_A, NMDA receptors.
**2. Voltage-Gated**
Activated by changes in membrane potential.
Drive action potentials and Ca²⁺ signaling.
Examples: Na⁺, K⁺, and Ca²⁺ channels in neurons and muscles.
**3. Metabotropic (Indirectly Gated)**
Controlled by GPCRs or other second-messenger cascades (cAMP, IP₃).
Slower but longer-lasting effects.
Examples: GABA_B receptor, muscarinic ACh receptor (M₂).

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](images/slide_34_img_47.png) {left=55.75 top=0.00 width=44.25 height=31.55}

![](images/slide_34_img_48.png) {left=55.75 top=31.55 width=44.25 height=68.85}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 12.87%; top: 8.42%; width: 42.88%; height: 83.17%;">

The human genome encodes about 800 functional GPCRs (4%) of the identified human proteins. 
detect and respond to many different types of signals, including odorants, tastants, neurotransmitters, hormones involved in glycogen and fat metabolism, growth factors, and even physical stimuli (photons of light). 
approximately 35% of all drugs used in humans are agonists or antagonists of GPCRs
at least 134 GPCRs are targets of drugs approved in the US and EU.

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 5.83%; width: 80.35%; height: 87.13%;">

The heterotrimeric guanine nucleotide—binding proteins (G proteins) are signal transducers that communicate signals from many hormones, neurotransmitters, chemokines, and autocrine and paracrine factors. The extracellular signals are received by members of a large superfamily of receptors with seven membrane-spanning regions that activate the G proteins, which route the signals to several distinct intracellular signaling pathways. These pathways interact with one another to form a network that regulates metabolic enzymes, ion channels, transporters, and other components of the cellular machinery controlling a broad range of cellular processes, including transcription, motility, contractility, and secretion. These cellular processes in turn regulate systemic functions such as embryonic development, gonadal development, learning and memory, and organismal homeostasis

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](images/slide_36_img_49.png) {left=19.26 top=0.00 width=80.74 height=94.07}

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 14.56%;">

**All GPCR signal transduction pathways share the following common elements:**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 21.01%; width: 80.35%; height: 71.29%;">

a membrane-imbedded receptor that contains seven membrane-spanning α helices;
a heterotrimeric G protein, which functions as a receptor-activated switch by
cycling between an active GTP-bound and an inactive GDP-bound state;
A membrane-bound effector protein; and 
proteins that participate in amplification and desensitization of the signaling pathway. Protein kinases and second messengers such as cAMP and Ca2+ often participate in GPCR-mediated signal transduction pathways.

These pathways usually have short-term effects in the cell by quickly modifying the activities of existing proteins, including enzymes and ion channels. Thus these pathways allow cells to respond rapidly to a variety of signals, whether they be physical stimuli such as light or hormonal stimuli such as epinephrine.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.37%; top: 3.66%; width: 80.35%; height: 21.97%;">

All G protein–coupled receptors have the same orientation in the membrane and
contain seven transmembrane α-helical regions (H1–H7), four extracellular
segments, and four cytosolic segments

</div>

![](images/slide_38_img_50.png) {left=44.62 top=26.01 width=53.10 height=47.98}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.37%; top: 27.61%; width: 26.13%; height: 69.64%;">

The human GPCRs are divided into several families. Members of each family are
especially similar to one another in amino acid sequence and structure. The
families differ from each other in the details of how ligands bind the receptors.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.22%; top: 2.71%; width: 82.79%; height: 13.16%;">

**Three principal classes of G protein–coupled receptors that bind their ligand in different ways.**

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](images/slide_40_img_52.png) {left=15.96 top=0.00 width=55.45 height=100.00}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 43.50%; top: 50.00%; width: 56.50%; height: 47.58%;">

Family A is the largest family in mammals, segments of several membrane-embedded α helices and extracellular loops form the ligand-binding site. That site is exposed to the exoplasmic surface, but can extend down toward the middle of the membrane bilayer. 

Example: the β-adrenergic receptor, which bind hormones such as epinephrine and norepinephrine. As an example, in binding of epinephrine (adrenaline) to the β-adrenergic receptor, side chains of 15 amino acids located in four transmembrane α helices and the extracellular loop E2 make noncovalent bonds with the bound ligand.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.51%; top: 62.05%; width: 82.49%; height: 37.95%;">

The glucagon receptor is a member of family B of the GPCRs. Its ligand glucagon is a 29-amino-acid peptide hormone secreted by the α cells of the pancreatic islets; as we will see later in this chapter, it acts on the liver to trigger glycogen breakdown and secretion of glucose into the bloodstream. While the glucagon receptor has the seven transmembrane α helices characteristic of GPCRs, it also has a large exoplasmic domain that is connected to the first transmembrane α helix. This exoplasmic domain binds tightly to the C-terminus of glucagon, positioning glucagon’s N-terminus to bind to the pocket formed, as in β-adrenergic and other GPCRs, by residues from several transmembrane helices

</div>

![](images/slide_41_img_53.png) {left=31.44 top=0.00 width=51.86 height=62.05}

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 0.00%; width: 37.37%; height: 41.80%;">

The glutamate receptor is an excitatory neurotransmitter receptor in family C of GPCRs. The long extracellular segment forms a sort of venus flytrap domain that clamps around and binds the glutamate ligand. Many members of this class of GPCRs dimerize through their venus flytrap domains.

</div>

![](images/slide_42_img_55.png) {left=61.59 top=67.78 width=30.26 height=22.21}

(Natural ligand binding)

![](images/slide_43_img_56.png) {left=33.94 top=0.00 width=50.00 height=49.32}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.88%; top: 51.38%; width: 82.12%; height: 46.86%;">

When unbound by ligand and in the inactive conformation (R), the receptor has no surface that is complementary to the G protein and thus cannot bind to it. The binding of the agonist induces major conformational changes and enables the receptor to bind to the subunit of the intact heterotrimeric G protein containing GDP.
Binding of the GPCR to the alpha subunit of G induces a change in the conformation of the GDP-binding site on Galpha, allowing a lobe of the protein to
move and thus release the GDP. Next because of its higher concentration in the
cytosol, GTP rapidly binds to the empty guanine nucleotide site in the subunit.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 16.51%;">

**GTP-Binding Proteins Are Frequently Used in Signal Transduction Pathways as On/Off Switches**

</div>

![](images/slide_44_img_57.png) {left=54.74 top=20.32 width=45.26 height=73.76}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 15.10%; top: 19.69%; width: 39.64%; height: 80.31%;">

Heterotrimeric G proteins directly bind to and are activated by GPCRs that function as guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs): trigger release of GDP, the G protein then
spontaneously binds GTP, favored by the high intracellular concentration
of GTP relative to that of GDP that induces conformational change involving  two highly conserved segments switch I and switch II, that allow the protein to bind to and activate downstream signaling proteins.

</div>

</div>

---

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 6.97%;">

**Deactivation**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.36%; top: 10.78%; width: 82.06%; height: 84.16%;">

After a brief time, the active G protein is shut off, converting back to the inactive form. It has intrinsic GTPase activity and slowly hydrolyzes the bound GTP to GDP (seconds to minutes) and, thereby altering the conformation of the switch I and switch II segments so that they are unable to bind to the target protein.

The rate of GTP hydrolysis determines the length of time the GTP-binding protein remains in the active conformation and is able to signal its downstream target proteins

Monomeric (often called low-molecular-weight) G proteins, including Ras and various Ras-like proteins such as Ran and Sar, do not directly bind to receptors but act as intermediate proteins in signal transduction pathways. Monomeric G proteins play crucial roles in many pathways that regulate cell division and cell motility. For this reason, mutations in genes encoding these G proteins frequently lead to cancer.

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

![](images/slide_46_img_58.png) {left=24.50 top=0.00 width=67.92 height=46.67}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 17.88%; top: 49.72%; width: 80.94%; height: 48.52%;">

The rate of GTP hydrolysis  is often modulated by other proteins. For instance, both GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs) and regulators of G protein signaling (RGSs) accelerate GTP hydrolysis. 
Many regulators of G protein activity are themselves controlled by extracellular signals.
Toxin of Vibrio cholerae penetrates the plasma membrane of cells, such as those that line the small intestine, and catalyzes a chemical modification of Galpha preventing hydrolysis of GTP. Constant hyperactivation leads to the loss of electrolytes and water from these intestinal cells into the intestinal lumen

</div>

</div>

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 20.32%; width: 80.35%; height: 73.96%;">

All effector proteins in GPCR signal transduction pathways are either membrane-bound ion channels or membrane-bound enzymes that catalyze the formation of one or more of the second messengers.
Humans have 21 different alpha subunits, 6 beta subunits, and 12 gamma subunits.
The 21 alpha subunits are encoded by 16 genes, several of which undergo alternative splicing. So far as is known, the different beta-gamma subunits are interchangeable, while the different alpha subunits provide specificity to the various G proteins.
GPCRs represent the largest protein family in the human genome- approximately 800 genese.
400 GPCR genes are thought to encode sensory receptors; of these, the majority are in the olfactory system and bind odorants.

</div>

Regulation of adenylyl cyclase activity, which occurs in many cell types, provides fine-tuned control of the cAMP level and thus of the downstream cellular response.
Virtually all of the diverse effects of cAMP are mediated through its activation of protein kinase A (PKA). This protein kinase phosphorylates multiple intracellular target proteins expressed in different cell types.

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 0.00%; width: 45.24%; height: 98.72%;">

Inactive PKA is a tetramer consisting of two regulatory (R) subunits and two catalytic (C) subunits. Each of the two R subunits in an R2 dimer has two cAMP-binding domains, called CNB-A and CNB-B, as well as a linker domain of unknown structure. At the C-terminus there is a dimerization domain that binds an AKAP protein. A segment of the CNB-A domain of the R subunit is a pseudosubstrate; its sequence resembles that of a peptide substrate and binds to the active site in the catalytic domain but is not phosphorylated. In the absence of cAMP the pseudosubstrate segment binds to this catalytic site and inhibits its activity.

Binding of cAMP to the CNB-A domain causes a change in its conformation so that it can no longer bind to and inhibit the catalytic subunit; this releases the C subunit, instantly activating its kinase activity.

**Example: Regulation of glycogen metabolism by cAMP and PKA**

![](images/slide_53_img_68.png) {left=51.49 top=14.14 width=48.51 height=42.63}

![](images/slide_53_img_69.png) {left=51.49 top=58.09 width=48.51 height=41.91}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 14.91%; top: 52.72%; width: 36.58%; height: 47.28%;">

**(a)** An increase in cytosolic cAMP activates PKA, which phosphorylates glycogen synthase (GS), inhibiting its catalytic activity to synthesize glycogen. Active PKA also promotes glycogen degradation via a protein kinase cascade that activates glycogen phosphorylase. At high cAMP concentrations, PKA also phosphorylates an inhibitor of phosphoprotein phosphatase (PP). Binding of thephosphorylated inhibitor to PP prevents this phosphatase from de-phosphorylating the
activated enzymes in the glycogen phosphorylase kinase cascade or the inactive glycogen synthase. 
**(b)** A decrease in cAMP inactivates PKA, leading to release of the active form of PP. The activation of PP promotes glycogen synthesis and inhibits glycogen degradation.

**CREB Links cAMP and PKA to**
**Activation of Gene Transcription**

Following the elevation of cAMP levels and the release of active PKA catalytic subunits, some of the catalytic subunits move into the nucleus. There they phosphorylate serine-133 on a transcription factor called CRE-binding (CREB) protein. CRE = AMP-response element, which
contains the highly conserved nucleotide sequence, 5’-TGACGTCA-3’. 

Phosphorylated CREB binds to CRE-containing target genes and also binds to the co-activator termed CBP/P300. CBP/P300 links CREB to RNA polymerase II and other gene regulatory proteins, thereby stimulating gene transcription.

AKAPs regulate cAMP and PKA signaling within the cell both spatially and temporally. One AKAP in heart muscle anchors PKA to the outer nuclear membrane. From this location, the released, catalytically active C subunits readily enter the nucleus, where they phosphorylate and activate the CREB transcription factor

<div style="position: relative; width: 100%; aspect-ratio: 16 / 9; margin: 0 auto;">

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 2.57%; width: 80.35%; height: 92.28%;">

The extracellular signaling molecules that activate receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) constitute a large class of soluble and membrane-bound protein hormones, including many that were initially identified because they stimulate the growth of specific types of cells (hence the name growth factor). 
Examples:
nerve growth factor (NGF),
platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), 
fibroblast growth factor (FGF),
epidermal growth factor (EGF)
Others, such as insulin, regulate expression of multiple genes that control sugar and lipid metabolism in liver, muscle, and adipose cells. The large family of ephrins play critical roles in regulating the migration of many nerve cell axons to their target destinations. Many RTKs and their ligands were first identified in studies of human cancers, in which mutant forms of growth-factor receptors
stimulate cell proliferation even in the absence of the growth-factor ligand. The mutation locks the receptor conformation in a state such that the kinase is permanently active. Other RTKs were uncovered during analysis of developmental mutations that lead to blocks in differentiation of specific cell types in Caenorhabditis elegans, Drosophila, and mice.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 12.74%; top: 54.88%; width: 86.43%; height: 44.51%;">

Most RTKs are monomeric, and ligand binding to the extracellular domain induces two monomers to join together as a dimer.
The cytosolic kinase domain of an RTK contains a flexible domain termed the activation loop. In the resting state, the activation loop is not phosphorylated and assumes a conformation that blocks kinase activity.
For most, but not all, RTKs, the weakly active kinase in each subunit is thought to phosphorylate a key tyrosine residue in the activation loop of the other subunit.
The strongly active kinase then phosphorylates additional tyrosine residues in the cytosolic domain of the receptor

**Phosphoinositide Signal Transduction Pathways**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 24.12%; width: 80.35%; height: 63.31%;">

RTK activation → autophosphorylation of tyrosine residues on the cytosolic domain.
Phosphorylated RTK recruits phospholipase C-γ (PLCγ) via SH2 domains.
PLCγ is activated by phosphorylation → cleaves PIP₂ (phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate) in the plasma membrane.
PIP₂ → IP₃ (inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate) + DAG (diacylglycerol).
IP₃ diffuses to the ER → releases Ca²⁺ into the cytosol.
DAG remains in the membrane → together with Ca²⁺, activates Protein Kinase C (PKC).
PKC phosphorylates various target proteins → modulates metabolism, gene expression, and cell growth.
Terminated by dephosphorylation of IP₃ and reconversion of DAG to PIP₂.

**Phosphoinositide Pathway via PI3K–Akt (PKB)**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 54.50%; width: 86.47%; height: 45.50%;">

RTK activation → recruits and activates phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K).
PI3K phosphorylates PI(4)P → PI(3,4)P in the plasma membrane, PI(3,4,5)P can be formed (PI-5 kinase).
PI(3,4)P serves as a docking site for Akt (PKB) and PDK1 via their PH domains.
PDK1 + mTORC2 phosphorylate and fully activate Akt.
Activated Akt (PKB) phosphorylates many targets controlling:
- Cell survival (inhibits apoptosis via Bad phosphorylation),
- Cell growth (via mTOR pathway),
- Metabolism (stimulates glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis).
Signal is terminated by PTEN, which dephosphorylates PIP₃ → PIP₂.

</div>

**JAK/STAT Signal Transduction Pathway**

![](images/slide_69_img_85.png) {left=63.87 top=0.00 width=36.13 height=52.57}

<div style="position: absolute; left: 13.74%; top: 50.00%; width: 86.26%; height: 50.00%;">

Janus kinases (JAKs) bound to receptor tails cross-phosphorylate each other
Activated JAKs phosphorylate tyrosine residues on the receptor’s cytoplasmic domain

Typical in immune and hematopoietic signaling

**TGF-β / Smad Signal Transduction Pathway**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 24.12%; width: 47.64%; height: 69.84%;">

TGF-β ligand binds a Type II receptor serine/threonine kinase

R-Smads form a complex with Smad4 (co-Smad)

**Notch/Delta Signaling Pathway**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 11.07%; top: 14.74%; width: 49.24%; height: 81.31%;">

**Contact-dependent signaling:** Both Notch receptor and Delta (or Jagged) ligand are membrane proteins — signaling occurs only between adjacent cells.
**Ligand binding and cleavage:** When Delta binds Notch, the receptor’s extracellular domain is cleaved by ADAM protease, then further cut by γ-secretase.
**Release of Notch intracellular domain (NICD):** The NICD fragment enters the nucleus.
**Transcriptional activation:** NICD binds the CSL (CBF1/Su(H)/Lag-1) transcription factor, converting it from a repressor to an activator. → Induces genes controlling cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis.

**Wnt/β-Catenin Signaling Pathway**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 13.55%; width: 37.22%; height: 84.28%;">

**Ligand and receptor: **Wnt proteins (secreted glycoproteins) bind to Frizzled (Fz) receptors and LRP5/6 co-receptors on the cell surface.

**Without Wnt: **β-catenin is continuously degraded by the “destruction complex” (Axin, APC, GSK3β, CK1). → Low cytosolic β-catenin → Target genes repressed by TCF/LEF.

**With Wnt: **Binding of Wnt to Fz/LRP inactivates the destruction complex via Dishevelled (Dsh) activation.→ β-catenin accumulates and enters the nucleus.

**Nuclear action: **β-catenin binds TCF/LEF transcription factors, converting them from repressors to activators of gene expression. → Induces genes controlling cell proliferation, differentiation, and fate determination.

Dysregulated Wnt signaling → cancer (e.g., colon carcinoma), fibrosis, and developmental defects.

**Hedgehog (Hh) Signaling Pathway**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 53.03%; width: 83.13%; height: 46.97%;">

**Ligand and receptors: **Hedgehog (Hh) family ligands — Sonic (Shh), Indian (Ihh), Desert (Dhh) — bind to the Patched (PTCH) receptor on the cell membrane.
**Without Hh: **PTCH inhibits Smoothened (SMO), a GPCR-like protein. GLI transcription factors (GLI2/3) are phosphorylated and processed into repressors (GLI-R).→ Target genes remain silent.
**With Hh present: **Hh binding relieves PTCH inhibition on SMO. Activated SMO initiates signaling that prevents GLI cleavage. Full-length GLI activators (GLI-A) accumulate and enter the nucleus.→ Transcription of Hh-responsive genes (e.g., involved in proliferation and patterning).

</div>

Aberrant activation → cancers (basal cell carcinoma, medulloblastoma).

**NF-κB signaling pathway**

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 11.74%; top: 50.35%; width: 87.26%; height: 50.00%;">

**Inactive state: **NF-κB is bound to IκB, sequestered in the cytoplasm → no transcription
**Activation:** Triggered by cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1), pathogen signals (via TLRs), or stress. IKK complex phosphorylates IκB, marking it for ubiquitination and degradation. Freed NF-κB translocates to the nucleus.
**Nuclear action: **NF-κB binds DNA and activates target genes for inflammation (e.g., cytokines, COX-2), cell survival (anti-apoptotic genes), immune responses.
**Termination / feedback: **NF-κB induces IκB gene transcription, which re-inhibits NF-κB → negative feedback loop

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 3.81%; width: 80.35%; height: 20.31%;">

**Signalosomes **are large supramolecular protein complexes that undergo clustering (oligomerisation or polymerisation) and/or colloidal phase separation to form biomolecular condensates that increase the local concentration and signalling activity of the individual components. They are an example of molecular self-assembly and self-organisation in cell biology.

</div>

<div style="position: absolute; left: 16.07%; top: 24.12%; width: 80.35%; height: 71.81%;">

**Wnt signalosome:** Transduction of Wnt signals from the plasma membrane depends on clustering of LRP6 receptors with Dishevelled (Dvl) proteins to recruit the Axin complex for inactivation.

**B-cell receptor (BCR) signalosome:** The B-cell receptor (BCR) binds antigen and undergoes clustering to induce signal transduction.

**T-cell receptor (TCR) signalosome:** Antigen presentation to T-cells is recognised by the T-cell receptor (TCR), which initiates clustering and activation of downstream signalling to induce T-cell responses.

**COP9 signalosome:** Catalyses the hydrolysis of NEDD8 protein from the Cullin subunit of Cullin-RING ubiquitin ligases (CRL). Therefore, it is responsible for CRL deneddylation – at the same time, it is able to bind deneddylated cullin-RING complex and retain them in deactivated form. COP9 signalosome thus serves as a sole deactivator of CRLs.

**RIP1/RIP3 Necrosome:** A signalling complex involved in necrotic cell death.

**Inflammasomes:** The AIM2 and NLRP3 inflammasomes are filamentous assemblies that elicit host defense inside cells by activating caspase-1 for cytokine maturation and cell death.
